## John Locke

**John Locke** · Wrington, England · 1632 – 1704 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Locke was born in 1632 to Puritan parents in Somerset, England. He grew up through the civil war, the execution of King Charles I, and the return of the monarchy. He studied at Oxford and stayed 15 years, drawn more to medicine and experiment than to the old curriculum.

In 1666 a chance meeting brought him into the household of Lord Shaftesbury, a leading politician. As his doctor, Locke oversaw a risky operation that Shaftesbury credited with saving his life. When Shaftesbury fell from favour, Locke fled to the Dutch Republic in 1683.

He came home after the Glorious Revolution of 1688 and published his major works within a year or two. He spent his last 14 years in the country house of his friend, the philosopher Lady Masham, and died there in 1704.

### What he cared about

Locke asked where our knowledge comes from and what makes a government legitimate. His themes:

- **The mind as a blank slate:** we are born without ready-made ideas; everything we know comes from experience.
- **Government by consent:** rulers hold power only with the people's consent, and a tyrant who loses it may be resisted.
- **Law protects freedom:** good law does not shrink our liberty; it preserves and enlarges it.
- **Toleration:** faith is a personal matter, and churches should not force belief.
- **New ideas deserve a hearing:** a truth is no less true for being new.

### What might surprise you

The "father of liberalism" was tied to the slave trade. He held shares in a company that traded enslaved Africans, and he helped draft the Fundamental Constitutions of Carolina, which gave colonists absolute power over their slaves. Historians still debate how far he shared those views.

He also kept his name off his own revolution. *Two Treatises of Government* and *A Letter Concerning Toleration* came out anonymously. Their ideas later shaped the language of the American Declaration of Independence.

### Where to start reading

*Second Treatise of Government*, chapters 2 to 9: the core of his case for freedom and consent. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## Denis Diderot

**Denis Diderot** · Langres, France · 1713 – 1784 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Diderot was born in 1713 in Langres, in eastern France, the son of a master cutler. He studied with the Jesuits and thought of becoming a priest, then tried law. When he chose to be a writer instead, his father cut him off, and he lived a poor, bohemian life in Paris for about ten years.

In 1749 his *Letter on the Blind* made his name and got him jailed for some months at Vincennes. From 1751 he led the *Encyclopédie* with d'Alembert, the great Enlightenment project to gather all human knowledge, including the crafts and trades. Church and state both banned it, and he kept it going for years.

The work left him poor. In 1766 Catherine the Great of Russia bought his library and paid him to look after it for life, and he later spent five months at her court. He died in Paris in 1784.

### What he cared about

Diderot was curious about everything, from machines to paintings to the morals of daily life. His themes:

- **The passions:** only strong feeling can raise the soul to great things, though reason must guide it.
- **Knowledge for all:** useful knowledge, from philosophy to the crafts and trades, should be shared.
- **Free thought:** he moved from belief in a distant God towards materialism and atheism.
- **Possessions own us:** buy one fine thing and you soon feel you must replace everything around it.
- **Art seen with fresh eyes:** his reviews of the Paris art exhibitions were among his most admired writing.

### What might surprise you

A dressing gown made him famous in economics. In a 1769 essay he describes how a gift of a fine scarlet robe made his old study look shabby, until he had replaced it all and fallen into debt. Today this is called the "Diderot effect".

Much of his best writing stayed in a drawer. *Rameau's Nephew*, *Jacques the Fatalist*, and *The Nun* were published only after his death. In private he was no model of virtue: he was unfaithful to his wife, and his letters to his lover Sophie Volland are among his finest writing.

### Where to start reading

"Regrets on My Old Dressing Gown": a few pages, light and very modern. Free online; the app uses its own translations from the French.

## Thomas Hobbes

**Thomas Hobbes** · Malmesbury, England · 1588 – 1679 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Hobbes was born in 1588 in Wiltshire, England, early, it was said, because his mother took fright at news of the Spanish Armada. He later joked that she gave birth to twins: himself and fear. His father left the family after a fight, and a wealthy uncle raised him.

After Oxford he became tutor to the Cavendish family, which gave him a living, a library, and travels across Europe. An early biographer says he met Galileo. He translated Thucydides into English straight from the Greek.

As England slid into civil war, he left for Paris in 1640 and stayed until 1651. There he taught mathematics to the exiled young Prince of Wales and wrote *Leviathan* (1651). He lived to 91, still writing, and was said to have called death "a great leap in the dark".

### What he cared about

Hobbes asked why people obey rulers at all, and what happens when they stop. His themes:

- **The state of nature:** without government, life is a war of all against all, "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short".
- **The social contract:** people agree to give up some freedom to a ruler in exchange for safety.
- **A strong sovereign:** after the horrors of civil war, he thought only a powerful ruler could keep the peace.
- **Peace makes thought possible:** leisure is the mother of philosophy, and an ordered state the mother of peace.
- **Fear and self-interest:** he saw them as the real drivers of human behaviour.

### What might surprise you

His view of people sounds bleak, but his argument is modern: political power comes from the people's agreement, not from God or birth. That made him enemies on all sides, and he was often accused of atheism.

He also kept working late into old age. In his mid-eighties he published an English translation of Homer's *Iliad* and *Odyssey*.

### Where to start reading

*Leviathan*, chapters 13 to 17: the state of nature and the birth of the state. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## Ibn Arabi

**Ibn Arabi** · Murcia, Spain · 1165 – 1240 · Persian and Sufi Poetry

### Who he was

Ibn Arabi was born in 1165 in Murcia, in Muslim Spain, and grew up in Seville, where his family served the ruler. As a boy he preferred military games to books. Then, as a teenager, he had a vision that turned him towards the Sufi path.

His father mentioned the change to the great philosopher Averroes, who asked to meet him. Ibn Arabi said the meeting taught him the difference between knowledge gained by reasoning and insight that comes by unveiling.

At 33 he left Spain for good. He travelled for 25 years, through North Africa, Mecca, Baghdad, and Anatolia, then settled in Damascus in 1223 to teach and write. He died there in 1240.

### What he cared about

Ibn Arabi was a mystic, philosopher, and poet who wrote in Arabic, not Persian: he belongs to this path as a Sufi master whose ideas spread across the Islamic world. About 800 works are attributed to him. His themes:

- **The unity of being:** everything that exists is a face of one single reality, God.
- **A heart open to every form:** a true lover can find the divine in a monastery, a temple, or the Kaaba.
- **Love as a religion:** he follows love wherever it leads.
- **Imagination:** not a trick of the mind but a real way of knowing the world.
- **The complete human:** a person who joins the divine and the created world in themselves.

### What might surprise you

His love poems are also theology. *The Interpreter of Desires* reads like a collection of classic Arabic love odes, yet he wrote a commentary explaining them as mystical poems. The famous lines on a heart that can hold every form come from it.

He was, and still is, contested. Many jurists saw him as a heretic, while Sufis called him "the Greatest Master" and some Muslims revere him as a saint. His ideas spread quickly across the Islamic world after his death.

### Where to start reading

*The Interpreter of Desires* (*Tarjumán al-Ashwáq*): short odes with his own notes. R. A. Nicholson's 1911 translation is free on Wikisource.

## Nicolas Chamfort

**Nicolas Chamfort** · Clermont-Ferrand, France · about 1741 – 1794 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Chamfort was born around 1741 in Clermont-Ferrand; sources differ on the date and raised by a grocer. Local tradition said he was the secret child of a noblewoman and a clergyman. At nine he went to Paris on a scholarship, and later lived for years on the hospitality of people who enjoyed his conversation.

Success came slowly, then all at once. His plays were performed before Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette, he received royal pensions, and in 1781 he was elected to the Académie française. But he disliked court life and kept stepping away from it.

When the Revolution came, he threw himself into it. He was among the first to enter the Bastille and served as secretary of the Jacobin club. Then his jokes turned on the new rulers, and during the Terror he was jailed. Threatened with arrest again, he tried to take his own life, and he died of his wounds months later, in April 1794.

### What he cared about

Chamfort left notes of maxims and anecdotes, published after his death as *Maxims and Thoughts*. His themes:

- **Laughter:** the most wasted day is the one without it.
- **Society's masks:** he watched the salons and the court closely and showed what lay behind the manners.
- **Freedom:** he hated servility, whether to a king or to a revolution.
- **Self-made knowledge:** he joked that he had forgotten what he was taught and only knew what he had guessed.
- **Books over people:** the great dead, he said, make better company than the living.

### What might surprise you

His wit cost him his life. A republican through and through, he could not stop mocking the revolutionary government, and it was his sarcasm, not royalism, that made him a suspect.

His maxims are often set beside La Rochefoucauld's. They are less polished but more violent, written in a time of storm. John Stuart Mill praised them highly.

### Where to start reading

*Maxims and Thoughts*: short and sharp, best read a few at a time. Free in French on Project Gutenberg; the app uses its own translations.

## George Eliot

**George Eliot** · Nuneaton, England · 1819 – 1880 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who she was

George Eliot was the pen name of Mary Ann Evans, born in 1819 in Warwickshire, the daughter of an estate manager. She was a voracious reader with a religious schooling. In her twenties, friends introduced her to writers who questioned the literal truth of the Bible. As she began to doubt, her father threatened to throw her out, and she kept house for him until his death.

Her first big work was a translation of a German book on the life of Jesus. In 1850 she moved to London, where she wrote and edited for the radical *Westminster Review*. She also translated Spinoza's *Ethics*.

From 1854 she lived openly with the writer George Henry Lewes, who was married to someone else. She began writing fiction at 37, and her novels made her one of the great writers of her age. She died in 1880, months after marrying her younger friend John Cross.

### What she cared about

Her novels look at ordinary people with deep attention and honesty. *Middlemarch* (1871–1872) is the best known. Her themes:

- **Sympathy:** we live to make life less difficult for each other.
- **The weight of ordinary pain:** if we truly felt all the suffering around us, we could not bear it.
- **Unhistoric acts:** much of the good in the world comes from quiet lives no one records.
- **Duty and work:** a good life is built from steady effort, not grand gestures.
- **Realism:** fiction should show people as they are, not as fantasies.

### What might surprise you

She chose a man's name partly to protect her private life. Living with a married man was a scandal, and many people cut her off. When she died, her lack of Christian faith and her life with Lewes kept her out of Westminster Abbey.

She was also a sharp critic of other women writers. Her essay "Silly Novels by Lady Novelists" (1856) mocked the empty plots of popular fiction. Virginia Woolf later called *Middlemarch* "one of the few English novels written for grown-up people".

### Where to start reading

*Middlemarch*: long, but it rewards every page. Start with the Prelude and the first book. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## Ralph Waldo Emerson

**Ralph Waldo Emerson** · Boston, United States · 1803 – 1882 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Emerson was born in Boston in 1803, the son of a Unitarian minister, and entered Harvard at 14. He became a minister himself. His first wife, Ellen, died of tuberculosis after less than two years of marriage, and two of his brothers soon followed.

After Ellen's death he grew more openly at odds with the church and resigned from the ministry in 1832. His essay *Nature* (1836) launched the movement known as Transcendentalism, and he became the friend and mentor of Henry David Thoreau.

He earned his living as a lecturer, giving some 1,500 talks across America, and turned many of them into essays. In his last decade his memory failed. He died in 1882, aged 78.

### What he cared about

Emerson believed each person has direct access to truth, if they dare to trust it. His themes:

- **Self-reliance:** trust yourself, and don't let conformity silence your inner voice.
- **Freedom to change your mind:** a foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds.
- **Your own thoughts:** in great works we recognise ideas we once had and dismissed.
- **Compensation:** every gain has a cost, and every loss a hidden gain.
- **Inner peace:** only living by your own principles brings it.

### What might surprise you

He shocked Harvard. In 1838 he told its divinity students that Jesus was a great man but not God. The address caused such a storm that the university did not invite him back to speak for 30 years.

He was also a quiet but firm opponent of slavery. He disliked the public eye, yet he lectured against it, denounced the Fugitive Slave Act, and welcomed the abolitionist John Brown to his home. Nietzsche called him "the most gifted of the Americans".

### Where to start reading

"Self-Reliance" (1841): one essay, about an hour's read, full of lines worth keeping. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## Erasmus

**Erasmus** · Rotterdam, Netherlands · about 1466 – 1536 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Desiderius Erasmus was born in Rotterdam, probably in 1466 or 1469; even he was unsure. His father was a Catholic priest, so his parents could not marry. Orphaned and poor as a boy, he entered a religious house and was later ordained a priest, though he never had much taste for the monastic life.

He spent his life on the move: Paris, England, Italy, Brabant, Basel, chasing patrons, manuscripts, and printers. In England he befriended Thomas More, and in London, while recovering from illness, he wrote *The Praise of Folly*, which became a best-seller.

In 1516 he published a landmark edition of the New Testament in Greek and Latin. When the Reformation split Europe, he stayed Catholic but argued for reform from within. He died in 1536.

### What he cared about

Erasmus was the leading scholar of the Northern Renaissance and wrote in a lively, natural Latin. His themes:

- **Laughing at vanity:** in *The Praise of Folly*, Folly herself mocks self-love and pride.
- **Wisdom at the right moment:** wisdom used out of place is just another kind of foolishness.
- **Back to the sources:** read the Bible and the ancient authors in their original words.
- **Peace:** real courage in a ruler is to curb greed, not to wage war.
- **Tolerance:** on matters that do not truly matter, Christians should live in concord.

### What might surprise you

His moderation made him enemies on both sides. He pressed for reform of the Church, yet stayed in it and openly broke with Luther over free will. His middle road disappointed and angered partisans in both camps.

He is remembered with a link to Rotterdam, but he lived there only about four years and never went back. Today his name lives on in the EU's Erasmus programme, which sends students to study abroad.

### Where to start reading

*The Praise of Folly* (1511): short, funny, and still sharp. Free on Project Gutenberg in White Kennett's old English translation.

## The Questions of King Milinda

***The Questions of King Milinda*** · India · about 100 BCE – 200 CE · Buddhist Philosophy

### What this text is

*The Questions of King Milinda* (in Pali, *Milindapañha*) is a Buddhist book in the form of a dialogue. A king asks hard questions about Buddhist teaching, and a monk named Nāgasena answers them, often with a simple image.

The king is real. Milinda is the Indian name of Menander I, a Greek king who ruled a large realm in what is now Pakistan and northwest India in the 2nd century BCE. Nāgasena is known only from this book and from later legend.

Scholars agree the work grew over time. Its earliest part was probably written between 100 BCE and 200 CE, and later authors added more. In Myanmar it is part of the Buddhist canon; in Thailand and Sri Lanka it is not.

### What it cares about

The king wants clear reasons, not just faith, and the monk gives them. Its themes:

- **No fixed self:** a chariot is not its wheels, pole, or axle; "chariot" is a name for parts working together, and so is a person.
- **Rebirth without a soul:** how can something be reborn if there is no lasting self to carry over?
- **Responsibility:** how our deeds follow us, and whether doing wrong knowingly is worse than doing it unknowingly.
- **Puzzles in the teaching:** dozens of apparent contradictions in Buddhist texts, taken one by one.
- **Teaching by image:** hard ideas made clear through everyday comparisons.

### What might surprise you

A Greek and an Indian sit down to argue philosophy. Yet scholars note that the dialogue shows little sign of Greek thought; its ideas trace back to Indian sources such as the Upanishads.

Buddhist tradition says the king was won over, handed his kingdom to his son, and left the world. A Greek source, Plutarch, says instead that he died in camp on campaign and that his cities shared out his ashes. Its 1890 translator, T. W. Rhys Davids, called it "the masterpiece of Indian prose".

### Where to start reading

Book II, the chariot dialogue: a few pages that sum up the Buddhist idea of the self. T. W. Rhys Davids's 1890 translation, in the *Sacred Books of the East*, is free online.

## Petrarch

**Petrarch** · Arezzo, Italy · 1304 – 1374 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Francesco Petrarca was born in Arezzo, in Tuscany, in 1304, and grew up mostly near Avignon, where the popes then lived. His father, a notary, made him study law, but he cared far more for Latin literature.

In 1327, in a church in Avignon, he saw a woman he called Laura. She became the subject of his love poems for the rest of his life, even after her death in 1348. His Latin epic *Africa* made him famous, and in 1341 he was crowned poet laureate in Rome.

He worked for the Church, travelled widely as a scholar and diplomat, and collected crumbling old Latin manuscripts. He spent his last years in the village of Arquà, near Padua, and died there in 1374.

### What he cared about

Petrarch wanted to bring the wisdom of ancient Rome back to life and use it to live better. His themes:

- **The classics:** his rediscovery of Cicero's letters helped start the Renaissance.
- **Knowing yourself:** in his *Secret* he argues with Saint Augustine about his own failings.
- **The struggle with our faults:** the pain of seeing them clearly and still not breaking free.
- **Love and longing:** his sonnets to Laura became a model for poets across Europe.
- **Curiosity:** he travelled for the pleasure of seeing, not only for duty.

### What might surprise you

He has been called "the first tourist". In 1336 he climbed Mont Ventoux in Provence just for the view, then opened Augustine's *Confessions* at the top and read it as a lesson about his own soul.

He also invented the idea of the "Dark Ages" for the centuries after Rome, a label most historians now find misleading. And though his Church career meant he could not marry, he had two children, a son and a daughter, whom he later recognised.

### Where to start reading

*Petrarch's Secret*: an imagined dialogue with Augustine, honest and searching. William H. Draper's 1911 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.
