## Topic page: Character and virtue

**Character and virtue** · theme: Character and virtue · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What makes a person good, and can you become one?

### How the traditions see it

**Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy).** Virtue, for Aristotle, is a settled trait of character, and you build it the way you learn a craft: by doing. We become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave ones. Each virtue sits between two extremes, like courage between cowardice and recklessness. Hitting that middle point is hard, and it takes practical wisdom.

**Confucius (Confucianism).** People start out much alike, Confucius said, and practice makes them grow far apart. The ideal is the "superior man", who asks a lot of himself and little of others, and who leads by example rather than by orders. Virtue is never lonely: whoever practises it will find neighbours.

**The Dhammapada (Buddhist Philosophy).** The *Dhammapada* puts the work in each person's hands: by oneself evil is done, and by oneself it is left undone. Small good acts add up, like a water jar filling drop by drop. Anger is overcome by love, and evil by good.

**Kant (Enlightenment Ethics).** Kant moved the focus from character to the will. Talents, courage, and even happiness can be used for bad ends; only a good will is good without qualification. Morality, he said, is not about how to be happy, but how to become worthy of happiness.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All four say goodness takes effort and is not simply given at birth. They split on where it lives. For Aristotle and Confucius, it is a character shaped by long practice and good company; for Kant, it is the intention behind each act, done out of duty. Virtue ethics, the tradition of Aristotle and Confucius, faded in the West after the Enlightenment and returned in the late 1950s.

## Topic page: Learning and knowledge

**Learning and knowledge** · theme: Learning and knowledge · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What is worth knowing, and how do we know that we know it?

### How the traditions see it

**Socrates and Plato (Classical Greek Philosophy).** Socrates claimed one kind of wisdom: he did not think he knew what he didn't. He questioned people until they saw the limits of their own knowledge, which made him many enemies. For Plato, philosophy begins in wonder, and most of us are like prisoners in a cave, taking shadows for the real thing.

**Confucius (Confucianism).** Confucius loved learning and said it should never stop. Learning without thinking is wasted, and thinking without learning is dangerous. Real knowledge means saying what you know and admitting what you don't. Anyone can teach you something: copy the good in people, and correct in yourself the bad you see in them.

**Laozi and Zhuangzi (Taoism).** The Taoists doubted that more knowledge brings more wisdom. Learning adds something every day, but the Way is found by letting go, day by day. Zhuangzi adds that you cannot explain the ocean to a frog in a well: we all see from a narrow place.

**Hume and Mill (Enlightenment Ethics).** Hume asked for less certainty and more evidence: a wise person matches belief to the evidence. Mill added that you only half know your own view if you have never heard the other side. Even a true belief, never challenged, turns into dead dogma.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Nearly all of them start with humility: Socrates, Confucius, and Laozi all praise knowing the limits of what you know. They part ways on what to do next. Confucius and Mill say study and argue more; Laozi says empty your mind; Hume says follow the evidence and stay modest about the rest.

## Topic page: Mind and emotions

**Mind and emotions** · theme: Mind and emotions · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Should we master our feelings, or listen to them?

### How the traditions see it

**Marcus Aurelius (Stoicism).** For the Stoics, what upsets us is not the event but our judgement about it, and we can learn to judge differently. Marcus Aurelius notes that the mind takes on the colour of its habitual thoughts. The ideal Stoic is free of disturbing passions, though not of all feeling.

**The Dhammapada (Buddhist Philosophy).** The *Dhammapada* opens with the claim that all we are comes from our thoughts. The mind is flighty and hard to hold, and taming it is the greatest good. Hatred never ends hatred; only love does.

**Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy).** Aristotle did not want feelings gone, but right. Anyone can get angry. The skill is to be angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, and for the right reason. A good character is one that feels the right amount.

**Spinoza and Hume (Enlightenment Ethics).** Spinoza called being ruled by our emotions a kind of bondage, and said an emotion loses its grip once we understand it clearly. Hume turned the question around: reason on its own moves no one to act, and is, in his words, "the slave of the passions".

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Most of them believe the mind can be trained, and that much suffering comes from how we react. They disagree on the goal. The Stoics and the Buddhist texts aim to calm or uproot troubling passions; Aristotle wants feelings in the right measure; Hume, like Pascal with his "heart" that has its own reasons, gives feeling the leading role.

## Topic page: Self-knowledge

**Self-knowledge** · theme: Self-knowledge · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Who are you, really, and can you ever know?

### How the traditions see it

**Socrates (Classical Greek Philosophy).** At his trial, Socrates said the unexamined life is not worth living. For him, questioning yourself and others was not a hobby but a duty, worth dying for.

**The Upanishads and the Buddhist texts (Indian Philosophy, Buddhist Philosophy).** The Upanishads look for a deep, lasting Self behind body and mind, and find it one with the Self in all beings. Buddhism turns this around. In *The Questions of King Milinda*, a chariot is just a name for its parts working together, and so is a person: there is no fixed self to find.

**Confucius (Confucianism).** For Confucius, self-knowledge is daily practice. One of his students examined himself every day on three points: had he been loyal, been true to his friends, and practised what he was taught? The superior man looks for the cause of things in himself; the petty man looks for it in others.

**Montaigne and La Rochefoucauld (Humanists and Essayists).** Montaigne made himself the subject of his *Essays*, because each person carries the whole human condition. La Rochefoucauld was less hopeful: we are so used to wearing masks for others that in the end we are disguised to ourselves.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

They all say the work begins inside, not in other people's opinions. They differ on what you find there: a lasting Self (the Upanishads), no fixed self at all (Buddhism), a self you still have to create (Nietzsche's "become what you are"), or a mirror of everyone (Montaigne).

## Topic page: Happiness and the good life

**Happiness and the good life** · theme: Happiness and the good life · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What does a good life need, and is happiness the point?

### How the traditions see it

**Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy).** Happiness, for Aristotle, is the one thing we want for its own sake. His word, *eudaimonia*, means living well, or flourishing, not a passing mood. It is judged over a whole life: one swallow does not make a spring, and one good day does not make a happy life.

**Epicurus (Epicureanism).** Epicurus made pleasure the goal, but not the pleasure of parties and luxury. He meant freedom from pain in the body and fear in the mind. Plain food, once hunger is gone, gives as much pleasure as a feast, and friends are the greatest help of all.

**Confucius and the Dhammapada (Confucianism, Buddhist Philosophy).** Both find happiness in contentment. Confucius said he could still find joy with coarse rice, water, and his bent arm for a pillow. For the *Dhammapada*, health is the greatest gift and contentment the best riches.

**Mill and Kant (Enlightenment Ethics).** Mill believed happiness is the goal, but some pleasures are higher than others: better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied. He also warned that happiness comes best on the side, while aiming at something else. Kant answered that morality is not about how to be happy, but how to become worthy of happiness.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Most agree that happiness is not pleasure piled up, and that wanting less helps. They disagree on whether happiness is the aim of life. For Aristotle, Epicurus, and Mill it is, in different forms; for Kant, doing right comes first, whatever it costs.

## Topic page: Ego, pride, and humility

**Ego, pride, and humility** · theme: Ego, pride, and humility · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

How much should we think of ourselves?

### How the traditions see it

**Laozi (Taoism).** The *Tao Te Ching* praises water: it helps everything and settles in the low places others avoid. A cup filled to the brim is hard to carry. Knowing others is clever, it says, but knowing yourself is wisdom.

**Confucius (Confucianism).** Confucius did not worry about being unknown, only about not knowing others. He noted that people of old studied to improve themselves, while people of his day studied to be seen. His students said he was free of four things, among them stubbornness and egoism.

**Epictetus (Stoicism).** The first task of anyone who studies philosophy, said Epictetus, is to drop self-conceit, because nobody can learn what they think they already know. He also asked his students to remember how small a part of the whole they are.

**La Rochefoucauld and Erasmus (Humanists and Essayists).** La Rochefoucauld called self-love the greatest of flatterers, and saw it behind even our virtues. In Erasmus's *Praise of Folly*, Folly herself admits that nothing is sillier than admiring yourself.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them see pride as a blind spot that keeps us from learning and from seeing others clearly. They differ on the cure. Laozi, Confucius, and Epictetus think practice can make us humbler. La Rochefoucauld doubts we ever fully escape self-love; at best we learn to notice it.

## Topic page: Change and impermanence

**Change and impermanence** · theme: Change and impermanence · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Everything changes, so how should we live with it?

### How the traditions see it

**Heraclitus and Lucretius (Classical Greek Philosophy, Epicureanism).** Heraclitus said you cannot step into the same river twice, because new water is always flowing. Lucretius saw the same motion in matter itself: nothing comes from nothing, and nothing returns to nothing; things only break apart and form again.

**The Dhammapada (Buddhist Philosophy).** For the *Dhammapada*, seeing clearly that all created things pass away is itself a path out of suffering. We suffer when we cling to what cannot last.

**Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius (Stoicism).** Epictetus advised never saying "I have lost it", only "I have given it back": a child, a wife, an estate were only lent to us. Marcus Aurelius put it in five words: the universe is transformation.

**Kenkō and Okakura (Japanese Aesthetics).** The Japanese writers find beauty in passing things. Kenkō asks whether we should look at the moon only when it is full, or at flowers only in full bloom. Okakura invites us to "dream of evanescence".

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them start from the same fact: nothing stays as it is. They differ on what to do with it. The Stoics accept it calmly; the Buddhist texts let go; the Japanese writers find beauty in it; Nietzsche goes further and welcomes change as the way we become new.

## Topic page: Desire and simplicity

**Desire and simplicity** · theme: Desire and simplicity · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Would we be happier wanting less?

### How the traditions see it

**Epicurus (Epicureanism).** Epicurus sorted desires into three kinds: natural and necessary, like drinking when thirsty; natural but not necessary, like costly food; and neither, like statues in your honour. The first are easy to satisfy; the last have no limit. Nature's wealth, he said, is small and easy to get.

**Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, and Seneca (Stoicism).** Marcus Aurelius advised doing few things if you want peace of mind. Epictetus put it more bluntly: don't admire your clothes, and you won't be angry with the thief. Seneca noted that we fear everything as if we were mortal, and want everything as if we would live forever.

**The Bhagavad Gita and the Dhammapada (Indian Philosophy, Buddhist Philosophy).** The *Gita* traces a chain: dwelling on things breeds attachment, attachment breeds desire, and desire breeds anger. Its answer is to act without clinging to the fruits of action. The *Dhammapada* says there is no fire like passion.

**Laozi and Thoreau (Taoism, Humanists and Essayists).** For Laozi, there is no greater calamity than never being content. Many centuries later, Thoreau went to live by Walden Pond to keep only the essentials of life. His advice: "Simplify, simplify."

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Most agree that endless wanting makes us unhappy, and that it is better to change our desires than the world. Not everyone wants to drop desire, though. Émilie du Châtelet wrote that we are happy only through tastes and passions that are satisfied, and Simone Weil saw desire as what leads the mind.

## Topic page: Time and the present

**Time and the present** · theme: Time and the present · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Is life short, or do we waste it?

### How the traditions see it

**Seneca (Stoicism).** Seneca argued that life is not short; we make it short by wasting it. How late, he said, to begin living just when we must leave. Epictetus added that he wanted to be present like the hour, and to pass like the hour.

**Epicurus (Epicureanism).** Epicurus warned against putting off joy for a tomorrow we do not control. The future is neither fully ours nor fully out of our hands. And remembering the good days we have had is part of a happy life.

**Omar Khayyam and Hafiz (Persian and Sufi Poetry).** The Persian poets feel time slipping past. Khayyam's "Moving Finger" writes and moves on, and nothing can make it go back. Their answer is to make the most of the days we have.

**Kenkō and Pascal (Japanese Aesthetics, Existential Thought).** Kenkō noted that the seasons come in order, but death comes when it likes, so we should not put off what matters. Pascal saw the same habit from another side: we never live, but only hope to live.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them say the present is the only time we truly have. They differ on what to fill it with: pleasure and wine for Khayyam, wisdom and duty for Seneca, quiet attention for Kenkō. Montaigne sums up the common ground: life is measured by its use, not its length.
