## Kenkō

**Kenkō** · Japan · about 1283 – 1350 · Japanese Aesthetics

### Who he was

Kenkō was the son of an official and served as an officer of the guards at the imperial palace in Kyoto. Later in life he left public life and became a Buddhist monk and hermit.

Nobody knows why. One story says it was an unhappy love; another, grief at the death of a former emperor. He kept writing poetry and took part in poetry contests at court, but his fame rests on one book, written between 1330 and 1332.

### What he cared about

*Essays in Idleness* is a collection of 243 short pieces, from a single line to a few pages. He wrote in a "follow the brush" style, letting one thought lead to the next. His main themes:

- **Impermanence:** everything passes, and that is part of its beauty.
- **Death:** it comes sooner than we think, so do not waste your days.
- **Nature and small things:** flowers before they bloom, a garden strewn with fallen ones, the moon as it wanes.
- **People:** friendship, manners, and the follies of those around him.

### What might surprise you

For a monk writing about death, Kenkō is often funny. He tells little stories from everyday life, like the archery teacher who tells a beginner to carry only one arrow, and he has firm views on taste: a little imperfection, he says, beats perfect sameness.

The book was not published in his lifetime and was not even divided into numbered passages until the 17th century. Even his name has a twist: "Yoshida Kenkō" comes from later documents that recent research has shown to be forged.

### Where to start reading

*Essays in Idleness*: open it anywhere and read one passage at a time. William N. Porter's 1914 translation, *A Miscellany of a Japanese Priest*, is free on archive.org.

## Fyodor Dostoevsky

**Fyodor Dostoevsky** · Moscow, Russia · 1821 – 1881 · Existential Thought

### Who he was

Dostoevsky was born in Moscow, trained as an engineer, and made his name with his first novel, *Poor Folk*, in the 1840s. In 1849 he was arrested for belonging to a circle that discussed banned books critical of the Tsar. He was sentenced to death, and the sentence was changed at the very last moment.

He then spent four years in a Siberian prison camp and six more as a soldier in exile. Back in St Petersburg, he wrote the novels that made him one of the greatest writers in any language: *Crime and Punishment*, *The Idiot*, *Demons*, and *The Brothers Karamazov*.

### What he cared about

Dostoevsky wrote novels, but they are full of ideas, argued by characters who live them. His main questions:

- **Freedom:** people want to choose for themselves, even against their own interest.
- **Suffering:** what it does to us, and whether it can have meaning.
- **Faith and doubt:** he was a Russian Orthodox Christian, and his novels put that faith through hard tests.
- **Responsibility:** each of us is, in some way, responsible for everyone.

### What might surprise you

The moment he faced the firing squad stayed with him for life, and he wrote it into *The Idiot*. Later, while travelling in Western Europe, he became addicted to gambling and at times had to beg for money.

His writings also contain negative stereotypes of Jews and other groups, and he denied being an enemy of the Jews when a reader accused him of it. Nietzsche called him the only psychologist he had learned anything from.

### Where to start reading

*Notes from Underground* (1864): short, bitter, and darkly funny, and one of the first works of existentialist literature. Constance Garnett's translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## The Dhammapada

***Dhammapada*** · India · sayings of the Buddha, 5th century BCE · Buddhist Philosophy

### What this text is

The *Dhammapada* is a collection of sayings of the Buddha in verse, and one of the most widely read Buddhist texts in the world. It has 423 short verses, grouped by theme into 26 chapters, such as thought, anger, happiness, and old age.

It belongs to the Pali Canon, the scriptures of Theravada Buddhism. Tradition holds that the Buddha spoke each verse on a particular occasion, in answer to something that had happened in his community. The title joins two words: *dhamma*, the teaching, and *pada*, path or verse.

### What it cares about

- **The mind comes first:** what we think shapes what we become.
- **Hatred never ends hatred;** love does.
- **Self-mastery:** conquering yourself is greater than conquering others.
- **Watchfulness:** "earnestness" in Müller's words: stay awake, and do not drift.
- **Kindness:** everyone fears pain and death, just as we do.

### What might surprise you

The verses are short, but they came with stories. A later commentary, linked to the scholar Buddhaghosa, gives the event behind each verse, and it is a rich source of legends about the Buddha's life.

Many verses work by contrast: joy and suffering, good and bad action, truth and lies, side by side. The *Dhammapada* is a religious text, and the app reads it here as practical wisdom that anyone can test.

### Where to start reading

Read a chapter at a time, in any order; the chapters on thought and on anger are good first steps. F. Max Müller's 1881 translation, made for the series *Sacred Books of the East*, is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Marcus Aurelius

**Marcus Aurelius** · Rome, Italy · 121 – 180 · Stoicism

### Who he was

Marcus lost his father at three and was raised by his mother and grandfather. In 138, the emperor Hadrian chose Antoninus Pius as his heir, and Antoninus in turn adopted Marcus. As heir to the throne, Marcus studied Greek and Latin with the best teachers of his time.

He became emperor in 161 and ruled until his death in 180. It was a hard reign: wars in the East and along the Danube, and a plague that killed millions across the empire. He is remembered as the last of the "Five Good Emperors".

### What he cared about

The *Meditations* are his private notes, written in Greek, many of them in army camps. He probably never meant them to be published. He keeps coming back to a few ideas:

- **Do your duty** today, well, without waiting for thanks.
- **We are made to work together,** like the hands and feet of one body.
- **Your mind is yours:** things cannot disturb you, only your judgments about them.
- **Everything passes,** including fame, so do not chase it.

### What might surprise you

The most powerful man in the world filled his notes with reminders to be patient and humble. Yet his reign was far from peaceful, and he spent years at war.

Persecution of Christians appears to have grown in his time, though historians think he was unlikely to have ordered it. He was also succeeded by his son, Commodus, and historians still debate that choice.

### Where to start reading

*Meditations*, Book II: short and direct, and it opens with his famous advice for the start of each day. George Long's 1862 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Rabindranath Tagore

**Rabindranath Tagore** · Calcutta, India · 1861 – 1941 · Indian Philosophy

### Who he was

Tagore was born into a leading family in Calcutta and wrote his first poems at eight. He became a poet, novelist, playwright, composer, painter, and teacher, and one of the key figures of the Bengal Renaissance.

In 1901 he founded a school at Santiniketan, which later grew into Visva-Bharati University. In 1913, after his own English version of *Gitanjali* moved readers like W. B. Yeats, he became the first non-European to win a Nobel Prize. He wrote some two thousand songs; two of them are now the national anthems of India and Bangladesh.

### What he cared about

- **Love as the way to freedom:** we become free not by escaping the world, but by loving it.
- **The unity of all life,** drawn from the Upanishads: the self, nature, and the divine are one.
- **Education in nature,** with art, music, and open-air classes.
- **One humanity:** he was a fierce critic of nationalism, in India and abroad.

### What might surprise you

King George V knighted him in 1915. Four years later, after troops under a British general fired on an unarmed crowd at Amritsar, killing hundreds, he gave the knighthood back in protest.

He wanted India free from British rule, yet he also argued with Gandhi, and sometimes criticised his campaigns. He travelled widely and met Einstein, Bergson, and H. G. Wells. At sixty, he also took up drawing and painting, and his works were shown across Europe.

### Where to start reading

*Stray Birds* (1916): tiny poems, often a single line, published in English. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## Confucius

**Confucius** · Qufu, China · about 551 – 479 BCE · Confucianism

### Who he was

Confucius, born Kong Qiu, grew up in the small state of Lu, in eastern China. His father died when he was three, and his mother raised him in poverty. He married at nineteen and had a son a year later.

In 501 BCE he became governor of a town, and later Minister of Crime in Lu. Later he left his home state for years, looking for a ruler who would put his ideas into practice. He came back to Lu at 68 and spent his last years teaching; tradition speaks of three thousand students.

### What he cared about

- **Good character,** built through learning and practice, at any age.
- **Do not do to others** what you would not want done to you.
- **Family and ritual:** respect, good manners, and ceremonies hold a society together.
- **Leaders as examples:** a ruler who is upright needs few orders.
- **Learning as joy,** shared with friends.

### What might surprise you

Confucius did not see himself as an original thinker. He said he was passing on the wisdom of earlier times, which his own age had forgotten.

One ancient book, the *Xunzi*, tells that soon after becoming Minister of Crime he had a rival scholar executed. Whether this really happened is uncertain, but it sits uneasily with his gentle image. He also rejected violent revolution, even against rulers who had taken power by force.

### Where to start reading

The *Analects*: short sayings and conversations with his students, collected after his death. James Legge's 1861 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Laozi

**Laozi** · China · traditionally 6th century BCE · Taoism

### Who he was

We are not sure. The earliest accounts say Laozi, "the Old Master", was a man called Li Er or Lao Dan, who kept the archives at the Zhou court and was an older contemporary of Confucius. Scholars are divided: some accept that a Lao Dan probably lived, others see him as largely or wholly a legend.

The best-known story says that, at the age of 80, he set off west to live as a hermit. At the last gate, a guard named Yinxi recognised him and asked him to write down his wisdom before leaving. What he wrote, the story goes, was the *Tao Te Ching*.

### What he cared about

- **The Tao:** the way of all things, which cannot be fully named.
- **Effortless action:** the wise act without forcing, and things get done.
- **Water as a model:** soft and low, it serves everything and wears down the hardest stone.
- **Less is more:** fewer desires, fewer possessions, fewer rules.
- **Quiet leadership:** the best rulers are barely noticed.

### What might surprise you

The oldest pieces of the *Tao Te Ching* ever found were written on bamboo strips buried around 300 BCE. They hold only about 40 percent of the text we read today, which suggests the book grew over time.

Later, Laozi became far more than a writer. From the Han dynasty on, religious Taoism worshipped him as a god, and the Tang emperors claimed him as their ancestor.

### Where to start reading

The *Tao Te Ching*: 81 short chapters, best read one at a time. James Legge's 1891 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Blaise Pascal

**Blaise Pascal** · Clermont-Ferrand, France · 1623 – 1662 · Existential Thought

### Who he was

Pascal was born in Clermont-Ferrand and lost his mother at three. His father, a tax official, taught him at home. He was a child prodigy: at 16 he wrote an important work on geometry, and at 19 he began building one of the first mechanical calculators.

He went on to shape the theory of probability, in letters with the mathematician Fermat, and to clarify how air pressure and the vacuum work; the unit of pressure is named after him. He was often ill, and died in Paris at 39.

### What he cared about

On the night of 23 November 1654, Pascal had an intense religious experience and wrote a short note about it to himself. After that, he turned from science to faith and the human condition. His questions:

- **Our place in the universe:** we are tiny and fragile, yet we can think, and that is our dignity.
- **Distraction:** we keep busy so that we never have to sit alone with ourselves.
- **The limits of reason:** the heart knows things that reason cannot prove.
- **The wager:** a famous argument that, faced with uncertainty, believing in God is the better bet.

### What might surprise you

His best-known book is not really a book. The *Pensées* ("Thoughts") are scraps of paper for a defence of Christianity that he never finished, edited by others and published in 1670, eight years after his death. Today they count among the masterpieces of French prose.

He also wrote one of the most quoted apologies in literature. In his *Provincial Letters*, he excused a long letter by saying he had not had time to write a shorter one.

### Where to start reading

The *Pensées*: short fragments, some just a sentence, others a page. C. Kegan Paul's 1885 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Adam Smith

**Adam Smith** · Kirkcaldy, Scotland · 1723 – 1790 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Smith was born in Kirkcaldy, a small port in Fife; his father died two months before he was born. He studied in Glasgow and at Oxford, then gave public lectures in Edinburgh that made his name, and became a close friend of the philosopher David Hume.

As professor of moral philosophy in Glasgow, he wrote *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759). Seventeen years later came *The Wealth of Nations* (1776), the book that made him "the father of economics".

### What he cared about

The app reads Smith the moral philosopher, not the economist. In his first book he asks how we come to judge right and wrong:

- **Sympathy:** however selfish we are, we care about what happens to others, and feel with them.
- **The impartial spectator:** we judge ourselves by imagining how a fair outside observer would see us.
- **Feelings shape happiness:** love is pleasant in itself, while hatred and anger poison a good mind.
- **No special moral sense:** our judgments of right and wrong grow out of this everyday fellow feeling.

### What might surprise you

The thinker best known for self-interest and the market began with a book about kindness and fellow feeling. Many scholars now read his two books as one project, not two opposite views of human nature.

His friends also found him comically absent-minded. He had odd habits of speech and walk, and was known to talk to himself, a habit he had had since childhood.

### Where to start reading

*The Theory of Moral Sentiments*, Part I: the opening chapters on sympathy. The original 1759 English is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Al-Ghazali

**Al-Ghazali** · Tus, Iran · about 1058 – 1111 · Persian and Sufi Poetry

### Who he was

Al-Ghazali was born near Tus, in north-eastern Persia, and studied in Nishapur. The powerful vizier of the Seljuk Empire took him under his wing and, in 1091, made him head of the Nizamiyya in Baghdad, the most prestigious teaching post in the Muslim world.

Four years later, at the height of his fame, he went through a deep personal crisis. He gave away his wealth, left Baghdad, and spent years in Damascus, Jerusalem, and Mecca, then in quiet seclusion back home. He returned to teaching only briefly, and died in Tus in 1111.

### What he cared about

- **Honest doubt:** in his autobiography, *Deliverance from Error*, he describes testing every source of knowledge until he found certainty.
- **Knowing is not enough:** he realised he had chosen status and ego over the inner life he wrote about.
- **The Sufi path:** purifying the heart through practice, not only through study.
- **Happiness:** knowing yourself is the first step to true happiness, the theme of *The Alchemy of Happiness*.

### What might surprise you

He was a gifted philosopher who wrote a famous attack on the philosophers. His *Incoherence of the Philosophers* took aim at thinkers like Avicenna who built on Aristotle and Plato.

Scholars still argue about its effect. Some blame it for a later decline of science in the Islamic world; others say that decline had other causes. His contemporaries gave him the title "Proof of Islam".

### Where to start reading

*The Alchemy of Happiness*: his own shorter Persian version of his greatest work. Claud Field's 1909 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.
