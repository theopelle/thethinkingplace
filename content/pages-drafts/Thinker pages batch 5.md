## Sa'di

**Sa'di** · Shiraz, Iran · about 1210 – 1291 · Persian and Sufi Poetry

### Who he was

Sa'di was born in Shiraz, in southern Persia. His father, who taught him and, it is said, a lifelong habit of tolerance, died while Sa'di was still a boy. As a teenager he left for Baghdad to study, and then spent nearly thirty years travelling across the Islamic world.

He came home to Shiraz in 1257, already famous for his poems. He lived through the Mongol conquests: he wrote laments for the fall of Baghdad in 1258, and also a poem honouring the new Mongol rulers. Persians still call him simply "the Master".

### What he cared about

His two great books, the *Bustan* (*The Orchard*) and the *Gulistan* (*The Rose Garden*), teach through short stories, many from the lives of kings, beggars, and travellers. Their lessons:

- **We are one family:** all people share one origin, so another's pain is our own.
- **Rulers serve the people,** as a shepherd serves his flock, not the other way round.
- **Contentment** is the only treasure worth asking for.
- **Patience** is bitter, but its fruit is sweet.
- **A kind lie** can be better than a truth that stirs up harm.

### What might surprise you

Many of his stories are told in the first person, as adventures of his own: being taken prisoner by the Crusaders in Syria, or even killing a temple priest in India. Scholars think many of these are unlikely, and probably invented or told as if by himself for effect.

He also travelled far in Western memory. Voltaire loved the *Gulistan* and liked his friends to call him "Sa'di", and the lines on all humans as one family hang on a carpet in the United Nations building in New York.

### Where to start reading

The *Gulistan*, chapter I, "On the Manners of Kings". Short stories with a lesson, in prose and verse. James Ross's translation, revised in 1900, is free online.

## The Upanishads

***The Upanishads*** · India · about 800 – 300 BCE and later · Indian Philosophy

### What these texts are

The Upanishads are Sanskrit texts that close the Vedas, the oldest scriptures of Hinduism. Earlier parts of the Vedas deal with hymns and rituals; the Upanishads turn inward, to meditation, consciousness, and the nature of reality. The name is often explained as "sitting down near": a student sitting by a teacher to receive knowledge.

About 108 are known. The dozen or so oldest, called the principal Upanishads, were passed down by heart for centuries. The *Brihadaranyaka* is among the most ancient. The app also reads the *Katha*, *Isha*, and *Mundaka*, composed probably in the last few centuries BCE.

### What they care about

- **The self and the whole:** the deepest self (*Atman*) and ultimate reality (*Brahman*) are closely linked, and in many readings one.
- **The good and the pleasant** are two different things, and the wise choose the good.
- **The self as a rider:** the body is the chariot, the intellect the driver, the senses the horses.
- **We become our desires:** as a person desires, so he acts, and so he becomes.
- **Seeing all beings in oneself,** and oneself in all beings.

### What might surprise you

They reached Europe by a long road. In 1656, the Mughal prince Dara Shukoh had fifty of them translated from Sanskrit into Persian. A French scholar then turned the Persian into Latin, and that Latin version is what Schopenhauer read. He called them the most rewarding and uplifting reading in the world.

One of the last things the poet W. B. Yeats published, in 1938, was a translation of ten principal Upanishads.

### Where to start reading

The *Katha Upanishad*: short, and built around a story: a boy, Nachiketa, meets Yama, the god of death in Hindu tradition, and asks him about the self. F. Max Müller's translation, in the *Sacred Books of the East*, is free online.

## Friedrich Nietzsche

**Friedrich Nietzsche** · Röcken, Germany · 1844 – 1900 · Existential Thought

### Who he was

Nietzsche grew up in Röcken, near Leipzig, the son of a Lutheran pastor who died when Friedrich was four. He was a brilliant student, and in 1869, at only 24, he became professor of classical philology at the University of Basel. He served briefly as a medical orderly in the Franco-Prussian War.

Ill for much of his life, he resigned in 1879 and lived as an independent writer, moving between Swiss mountain summers and Italian and French winters, often alone and short of money. In January 1889, in Turin, he suffered a mental breakdown from which he never recovered. He spent his last eleven years in the care of his mother and his sister.

### What he cared about

Nietzsche wrote in aphorisms and poetic books like *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*. His big themes:

- **"God is dead":** with religion weakening, we must find new values and affirm life.
- **Become who you are:** create your own path instead of following the crowd.
- **Live dangerously:** growth comes from challenge, not comfort.
- **A friend should also be your best enemy,** pushing you to be better.
- **Eternal return:** could you love your life enough to live it again, forever?

### What might surprise you

His sister, Elisabeth, took control of his writings and edited them to fit her own nationalist views. Through her editions, his name became linked with fascism and the Nazis, though he had openly opposed antisemitism and nationalism. Later scholars restored his texts.

He never married. He proposed three times to the writer Lou Salomé, and was turned down each time.

### Where to start reading

*The Joyful Wisdom* (also called *The Gay Science*), Book IV: short sections, among his warmest writing. Thomas Common's translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Lucretius

**Lucretius** · Italy · about 99 – 55 BCE · Epicureanism

### Who he was

We know almost nothing about Lucretius's life. The only sure fact is that he was a friend, or a client, of a Roman politician named Gaius Memmius, to whom he dedicated his one surviving work. He lived in the last, violent years of the Roman Republic, in the age of Cicero.

That work is *On the Nature of Things*, a long poem in Latin that sets out the philosophy of Epicurus. It shaped the great Roman poets who came after him, above all Virgil.

### What he cared about

Lucretius used poetry to carry Epicurus's ideas to Roman readers. His aim was to free them from fear:

- **Nothing comes from nothing,** and nothing ever returns to nothing.
- **The world is made of tiny particles** moving in empty space, always coming together and falling apart.
- **Fear comes from ignorance:** people blame the gods for what they do not understand.
- **Death is no evil:** leave life like a guest who has eaten well at a banquet.
- **Everything changes:** nations rise and fall, and drops of water wear away stone.

### What might surprise you

The poem was almost lost. It survived the Middle Ages in very few copies until an Italian book hunter, Poggio Bracciolini, found one in a German monastery in 1417. It then fed the revival of atomism in early modern science, and Montaigne owned and annotated a copy.

A strange legend, recorded by Saint Jerome four centuries later, says Lucretius was driven mad by a love potion and took his own life. Scholars doubt it, not least because the same ancient notes get other basic facts about him wrong.

### Where to start reading

*On the Nature of Things*, Book I, especially the opening sections on atoms and fear. William Ellery Leonard's 1916 verse translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Immanuel Kant

**Immanuel Kant** · Königsberg, Russia · 1724 – 1804 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Kant was born in Königsberg, in Prussia, the fourth of nine children in a strict and devout Pietist family. After his father's death interrupted his studies, he worked for some years as a private tutor in towns around Königsberg, then returned to lecture at its university, where he became a professor in 1770.

He was a popular teacher and a sociable man, but he lived by a strict routine: it was said that neighbours set their watches by his daily walk. He twice thought of marrying, waited too long both times, and stayed single. His *Critique of Pure Reason* (1781) made him one of the central thinkers of the Enlightenment.

### What he cared about

Kant wanted to show what we can know, and what we must do. On ethics, his key ideas:

- **The only thing good without condition** is a good will.
- **Treat people as ends,** never only as means, yourself included.
- **Morality is not about being happy,** but about becoming worthy of happiness.
- **Duty from reason:** the moral law binds every rational being, whatever their wishes.
- **Awe:** the starry sky above us and the moral law within us.

### What might surprise you

The philosopher of human dignity also wrote openly racist things, and denied women full capacity for moral reasoning. Few scholars dispute this; they debate how much it undermines the rest of his work.

He also thought hard about peace. In *Towards Perpetual Peace* (1795) he argued that treaties alone cannot end wars: lasting peace needs republican constitutions, international law, and a free federation of states.

### Where to start reading

*Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, First Section: short, and the clearest way into his ethics. Thomas Kingsmill Abbott's translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Margaret Fuller

**Margaret Fuller** · Cambridge, United States · 1810 – 1850 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who she was

Fuller was born in Cambridgeport, Massachusetts. Her father, a lawyer, gave her an education as demanding as any boy's: she was translating Virgil from Latin as a small child. By her thirties she was known as the best-read person in New England, and she was the first woman allowed to use Harvard's library.

From 1839 she ran "Conversations", discussion classes for women who had no access to higher education. Emerson made her the first editor of the Transcendentalist journal *The Dial*. In 1844 she joined the *New-York Tribune* as a critic, the first full-time book reviewer in American journalism.

### What she cared about

Her book *Woman in the Nineteenth Century* (1845) is considered the first major feminist work in the United States. Its key points:

- **Every path should be open to women** as freely as to men.
- **Self-reliance is for everyone:** independence praised in men is called a fault in women.
- **Growth:** people, women and men, are made to expand, not to be held in narrow roles.
- **Education:** women deserve the same chance to learn and think as men.

### What might surprise you

In 1846 the *Tribune* sent her to Europe as its first female foreign correspondent. In Italy she joined the side of the 1848 revolutions. During the siege of Rome she volunteered in hospitals, while her partner, Giovanni Ossoli, fought on the walls.

In 1850 she sailed home with Ossoli and their child. The ship sank off Fire Island, near New York, and all three died; her body was never found. The leaders of the women's suffrage movement later wrote that she had more influence on American women's thinking than any woman before her.

### Where to start reading

*Woman in the Nineteenth Century*: bold and still fresh. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## The Bhagavad Gita

***The Bhagavad Gita*** · India · likely 2nd or 1st century BCE · Indian Philosophy

### What this text is

The *Bhagavad Gita*, "the Song of God", is a poem of 700 verses in 18 chapters, set inside the great Indian epic, the *Mahabharata*. Tradition credits it to the sage Vyasa; scholars think several authors probably shaped it.

It is a dialogue on a battlefield. The prince Arjuna sees relatives, friends, and teachers in the enemy army, drops his bow, and does not want to fight. His charioteer, Krishna, whom Hindu tradition sees as a form of the god Vishnu, answers his doubts. Among Hindu traditions, it is the most widely revered sacred text.

### What it cares about

- **Act without clinging to results:** your concern is the action, never its fruits.
- **Do your own duty,** even imperfectly, rather than someone else's well.
- **A steady mind:** stay calm in pleasure and pain, success and failure.
- **The self as friend:** raise yourself by yourself; your own self can be your best friend or your enemy.
- **Moderation:** neither too much nor too little, in food, sleep, or effort.

### What might surprise you

Readers have argued for a long time about its setting. Some read it literally, as a call to fight; others, including Emerson and Walt Whitman, read the battle as a picture of the struggle inside each person. Gandhi learned verses of its second chapter by heart and called them a companion in his non-violent movement.

Its first English translation appeared in 1785. In 1965, J. Robert Oppenheimer, who had led the making of the atomic bomb, recited a verse from it in a television documentary.

### Where to start reading

Chapter II, which sums up the whole teaching. Kashinath Trimbak Telang's 1882 translation and Edwin Arnold's verse version, *The Song Celestial* (1885), are free online.

## Voltaire

**Voltaire** · Paris, France · 1694 – 1778 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Voltaire, born François-Marie Arouet in Paris, was educated by the Jesuits. His sharp tongue got him into trouble early: a satirical verse about the regent earned him eleven months in the Bastille, and he was later sent into exile in England, where he admired English freedoms and Newton's science.

From 1733 he lived for years with Émilie du Châtelet at Cirey. He spent time at the court of Frederick the Great of Prussia, and later settled at Ferney, near Geneva, on the French border, safely out of reach of the French king. He died in 1778, at 83.

### What he cared about

Voltaire wrote plays, histories, tales, and thousands of letters, always with wit. His causes:

- **Tolerance:** we are all weak and wrong, so let us forgive one another.
- **Freedom of speech and religion,** and the separation of church and state.
- **Against fanaticism:** he urged his friends to "crush the infamous", the abuses of church and crown.
- **Against easy optimism:** in *Candide* (1759) he mocked the idea that all is for the best.
- **Justice:** better to risk saving a guilty person than to condemn an innocent one.

### What might surprise you

From 1762 he used his fame to defend victims of injustice. The most famous was Jean Calas, a Protestant merchant tortured to death on the charge of killing his son to stop him becoming a Catholic. Voltaire took up the case and wrote his *Treatise on Tolerance* about it.

The champion of tolerance also had blind spots. He wrote with open hostility about Jews, and he supported the theory that human races had separate origins. Some historians link these views to his investments in colonial companies involved in the slave trade, though he also mocked slavery in *Candide*.

### Where to start reading

*Candide*: short, fast, and still funny. Free on Project Gutenberg in several translations; the app uses its own.

## Kamo no Chōmei

**Kamo no Chōmei** · Japan · about 1155 – 1216 · Japanese Aesthetics

### Who he was

Chōmei was the second son of the head priest of the Lower Kamo Shinto shrine in Kyoto. He grew up at court, loved music, and was known as a fine player of the *biwa*, a Japanese lute. In his thirties his poems won contests and appeared in anthologies, and the retired emperor Go-Toba appointed him to his Imperial Poetry Office.

But he lost his political backing and was passed over for the post at his family's shrine; a cousin got it instead. In 1204 he turned his back on society, took Buddhist vows, and became a hermit. After a few years at the foot of Mount Hiei, he built a tiny hut in the hills of Hino, south-east of the capital, and lived there for the rest of his life.

### What he cared about

In the hut he wrote *Hōjōki*, *An Account of a Ten-Foot-Square Hut* (1212). It opens with a river that never stops flowing and is never the same water. His themes:

- **Impermanence:** people and houses are like foam on the water, here and gone.
- **Disaster:** fires, a whirlwind, famine, and an earthquake that struck Kyoto in his lifetime.
- **Owning less:** a small, movable hut is enough, and frees you from worry.
- **Solitude:** music, poems, and walks in the hills as company.

### What might surprise you

He was an unusual hermit for his time. Most people who left the world joined a monastery; Chōmei lived alone instead.

He is also more human than his serene book suggests. He still held a grudge against the cousin who took the shrine post. And though he writes in *Hōjōki* that he never left his hut, another account says he made at least one journey away from it.

### Where to start reading

*Hōjōki*: only a few dozen pages. W. G. Aston's 1899 translation, in *A History of Japanese Literature*, is free online.

## Mary Wollstonecraft

**Mary Wollstonecraft** · London, England · 1759 – 1797 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who she was

Mary Wollstonecraft was born in 1759 in Spitalfields, London. Her father was a violent man who beat her mother in drunken rages. As a teenager, Mary would lie outside her mother's bedroom door to protect her.

She had few options as a poor but respectable woman. She ran a school at Newington Green with her sisters and her friend Fanny Blood, then worked as a governess in Ireland. Back in London, the publisher Joseph Johnson gave her work as a translator and reviewer, and she began to live by her pen.

Her reply to Edmund Burke, *A Vindication of the Rights of Men* (1790), made her famous overnight. Two years later came *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*. She went to revolutionary Paris, had a daughter with the American Gilbert Imlay, and tried to take her own life after he left her.

She then married the philosopher William Godwin. She died in 1797, aged 38, eleven days after giving birth to Mary Shelley, the future author of *Frankenstein*.

### What she cared about

She believed women seemed weaker than men only because they were denied a real education. Her themes:

- **Reason has no sex:** men and women are both rational beings and should be treated as such.
- **Education for all:** girls and boys taught together, in the same way.
- **Partners, not ornaments:** a wife should be her husband's companion, not property traded in marriage.
- **Independence:** a woman who depends on others for her body and her bread will soon depend on them for her thoughts.
- **Liberty makes people better:** freer societies make people wiser and more virtuous, women included.

### What might surprise you

Her most famous book was written to change one man's mind. In 1791 the French statesman Talleyrand proposed that girls receive only a domestic education. She also attacked Rousseau, who argued in *Emile* that women should be educated to please men.

She was not a modern feminist in every way. It is unclear whether she wanted women to vote, she wrote mainly for the middle class, and she could be harsh about the women of her day, calling the frivolous ones spaniels and toys.

After her death, Godwin published a frank memoir of her unconventional life. It wrecked her reputation for almost a century, until the feminist movement rediscovered her.

### Where to start reading

*A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*: long and passionate, but the opening chapters set out all its main topics. Free on Project Gutenberg; the app quotes her original English.
