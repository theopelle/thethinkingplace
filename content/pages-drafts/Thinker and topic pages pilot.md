# Thinker and topic pages: pilot

Oct 29, 2026 · @Théo

Five pilot pages to settle length, tone and shape before writing the rest: three thinkers, one concept and one society topic.

## The page shapes

A thinker page runs about 300 words; a topic page about 350. Both open from a card ("More about Seneca →") and make sense before the reader has met a single thought.

**Thinker page**

1. **Who they were:** 3–4 sentences.
2. **What they cared about:** 2–3 core ideas, a short paragraph each.
3. **What might surprise you:** one personal view or fact, said plainly, including the uncomfortable ones where they matter.
4. **Where to start reading:** one free, public-domain edition, the one the app already links to.
5. **Your thoughts and notes** (built by the app, not written): the thinker's thoughts you have met, with dates and your notes. Hidden while there are none; never a count or a list of what's missing.
6. **Sources:** the pages used, at the end.

**Topic page** (the app's themes, plus society topics like justice)

1. **The question in one line.**
2. **How the traditions see it:** 3–4 views, one short paragraph each, from the paths the app reads.
3. **Where they agree, and where they don't:** two or three sentences.
4. **Your thoughts and notes** on this theme (built by the app).
5. **Sources.**

**Rules:** facts only from the listed sources, written in our own words; quotes only from the app's checked thoughts; religion described neutrally (no God talk beyond what is already approved); plain English, short sentences.

## Thinker page: Seneca

**Seneca** · Córdoba, Spain · about 4 BCE – 65 CE · Stoicism

### Who he was

Lucius Annaeus Seneca was born in Corduba, in Roman Spain, and grew up in Rome. He became a well-known speaker and writer. In 41 CE he was exiled to Corsica on an adultery charge that many historians doubt.

Eight years later, Agrippina brought him back to teach her son, the future emperor Nero. When Nero came to power in 54, Seneca became one of his closest advisers and, for some years, one of the most powerful men in Rome. He stepped back around 62. In 65, after a plot against Nero failed, the emperor ordered him to take his own life, although his part in the plot is unlikely.

### What he cared about

For Seneca, philosophy was a cure for the mind, not a theory. He wrote less for the perfect wise person than for people still trying to improve. His key ideas:

- **Time** is our only real possession, and we waste it.
- **Anger** and other strong emotions come from our own judgments, so reason can calm them.
- **Death** is a natural law, and thinking about it helps us live well now.
- **Each evening**, look back on your day honestly, without harshness.

### What might surprise you

Seneca praised a simple life while being one of the richest men in Rome, partly through money lending. Critics in his own time attacked him for it; he answered that wealth is fine if you do not depend on it.

He also served Nero, and one of his essays praises the young emperor's mercy. Readers still argue about whether he was a hypocrite or someone who saw his own weaknesses clearly. Some of his remarks about women also reflect the views of his time.

### Where to start reading

*On the Shortness of Life*: short, clear, and still sharp. Then the *Letters to Lucilius*, 124 letters written in his last years. Aubrey Stewart's translation is free on Project Gutenberg. His tragedies, such as *Medea* and *Thyestes*, later influenced Shakespeare.

## Thinker page: Zhuangzi

**Zhuangzi** · China · about 369 – 286 BCE · Taoism

### Who he was

Zhuang Zhou, called Zhuangzi ("Master Zhuang"), came from the town of Meng in the state of Song. He lived during the Warring States period, a time of war and of many rival schools of thought, and was a contemporary of Mencius.

We know very little about his life. The historian Sima Qian says he held a minor official post and turned down a high position offered by the king of Chu because he valued his freedom. Most other stories about him come from the book that carries his name.

### What he cared about

Zhuangzi doubted that any single point of view can hold the whole truth. Each of us sees from where we stand, like the frog at the bottom of a well. His main ideas:

- **The Way** (Dao) cannot be fully known or put into words, and it is always changing.
- **Flexible minds** do better than fixed ones: stop clinging to one view and respond to what is in front of you.
- **Skill** at its best feels effortless, like the cook whose knife never goes dull.
- **The useless** can thrive: the tree nobody wants to cut down grows old.
- **Death** is part of nature's constant change, not something to fear.

### What might surprise you

Zhuangzi probably did not write most of the book. Only the first seven chapters, the Inner Chapters, are usually credited to him; the rest came from later followers with quite different views.

He is also funny. He puts words in the mouth of Confucius, Taoism's great rival, and lets him say Taoist things. When his wife died, a friend found him drumming on a pot and singing, and was shocked. Even an ancient chapter of the book criticises him for ignoring practical matters.

### Where to start reading

The Inner Chapters, especially chapter 2 (with the butterfly dream) and chapter 3 (the cook). Short, strange, and full of stories. The translations by Herbert Giles (1889) and James Legge (1891) are free online.

## Thinker page: Émilie du Châtelet

**Émilie du Châtelet** · Paris, France · 1706 – 1749 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who she was

Émilie du Châtelet was born in Paris into a noble family. Her father let her discuss astronomy with the writer Fontenelle when she was ten, and by twelve she read Latin, Italian, Greek, and German. She married a marquis at eighteen; after three children, the couple agreed to live separate lives.

From 1733 she studied mathematics with leading scientists, and from 1734 she lived with Voltaire at Cirey, her family's country house, where they set up a laboratory. In 1738 she secretly entered a science prize of the Académie des Sciences on the nature of fire. Her essay was published by the Académie, the first work by a woman it printed.

### What she cared about

She wanted to understand how the world works, and to give physics solid foundations. Her main work:

- **Foundations of Physics** (1740) tried to bring together the ideas of Newton and Leibniz.
- **Energy:** she argued that a moving body's force grows with the square of its speed, a key step towards the modern idea of energy.
- **Newton's Principia:** her French translation, with commentary, is still the standard one.
- **Happiness:** in her *Discourse on Happiness*, she says we need passions we can satisfy and freedom from prejudice, and that the love of study is the most reliable source of joy.

### What might surprise you

For a long time, history remembered her mainly as Voltaire's partner, not as a scientist in her own right. Only in recent decades have scholars judged her work on its own terms.

She was also a keen gambler: once she lost a large sum in one evening at court, to card cheats. At 42 she became pregnant, feared she would not survive, and kept working to finish her Newton translation. She died six days after giving birth.

### Where to start reading

The *Discourse on Happiness*: short, personal, and honest. She wrote it in the 1740s, and it was only published in 1779, thirty years after her death. The French original is free on Wikisource.

## Topic page: Friendship

**Friendship** · theme: Friendship and kindness · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What makes someone a real friend, and why do we need them?

### How the traditions see it

**Aristotle (Ancient Greece).** He saw three kinds of friendship: for usefulness, for pleasure, and for character. The first two fade when the use or the fun ends. Only the third lasts, because each friend loves who the other is. Such a friend, he says, is "another self", and good friends make each other better. He thought nobody would choose a life without friends, even with everything else.

**Epicurus (Ancient Greece).** For Epicurus, friendship was the greatest help to a happy life, worth more than wealth or power. He lived it: his school, the Garden, was a community of friends outside politics, who shared meals and celebrations, and it welcomed women as students.

**Confucius (Ancient China).** Friends help each other grow in virtue. A good friend tells you honestly when you are wrong, and does it with tact. But if the friend will not listen, you stop, rather than lose the friendship.

**Montaigne (the Humanists).** After his friend Étienne de La Boétie died young, Montaigne wrote that true friendship cannot be explained: it happens between these two people, and no others. He believed such a friendship is so complete that a person can have only one, and that it is very rare. In the same essay he says women had never reached this kind of friendship, a view common among writers of his time.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All four see friendship as one of the best things in life, and all link it to becoming a better person. They differ on numbers and reasons. Epicurus spoke openly of the safety and pleasure friends give; Aristotle cared most about friends valued for themselves; Montaigne thought the deepest kind happens only once. Nietzsche adds a sharper voice: a true friend should also challenge you.

## Topic page: Justice (society)

**Justice** · theme: Justice, power, and leadership · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What do we owe each other, and who decides?

### How the traditions see it

**Plato and Socrates (Ancient Greece).** In Plato's *Republic*, a character called Thrasymachus says justice is simply what serves the strong. Plato's answer is that justice is a kind of harmony: each part of a person, or of a city, doing its proper job. His teacher Socrates went further. He said it is better to suffer an injustice than to do one, and that we should never return harm for harm.

**Aristotle (Ancient Greece).** For Aristotle, justice means giving people what they deserve, and good laws are reason without passion. A citizen is someone who takes turns ruling and being ruled. Yet the same book, the *Politics*, argues that some people are slaves by nature. Centuries later, this idea was used to defend the enslavement of Native Americans.

**Epicurus (Ancient Greece).** Epicurus saw no justice written into nature. For him, justice is an agreement between people not to harm each other, and it can change from place to place. It still matters to the individual: the just person lives with the most peace of mind.

**Confucius (Ancient China).** Justice begins with the people who lead. A ruler who acts rightly will be followed without needing many orders. When someone wrongs you, answer with justice, not with kindness or revenge. In classical Confucian thought, justice was a personal and public virtue, not a matter of law or equal rights.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Most agree that justice is about more than rules: it shapes the kind of person you become. They split on where it comes from. For Plato it is part of how things should be; for Epicurus, and later for social contract thinkers like Rousseau, it is something people agree on. Modern debates add new questions the ancients rarely asked, such as how fair inequality can be, as in John Rawls's "veil of ignorance", or whether punishment should aim to repair harm.

## Topic page: Democracy (society)

**Democracy** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Should the people rule, and what keeps the many from going wrong?

### How the traditions see it

**Athens and Plato (Ancient Greece).** Democracy began in Athens around 508 BCE. Citizens voted directly in an assembly, and many officials and jurors were chosen by lot. But only adult male citizens took part, perhaps one person in five or fewer: women, slaves, and foreigners were left out.

Plato did not trust it. In his parable of the ship, the sailors fight over the helm, while the true pilot, who knows the winds and the stars, is mocked as a stargazer. He had seen a democratic jury sentence his teacher Socrates to death in 399 BCE.

**Aristotle (Ancient Greece).** Aristotle was more open. A citizen, for him, is someone who takes turns governing and being governed, and a crowd can judge many things better than one person. Still, he feared rule by the many could turn into mob rule. He preferred a mixed government, held steady by a strong middle class.

**Laozi (Taoists).** The *Tao Te Ching* looks at power from below. The best rulers are those the people hardly notice; worse ones are loved, then feared, then despised. Good government works quietly and lets people feel they did things themselves.

**Rousseau and Mill (the Enlightenment and after).** Rousseau put the people at the heart of the state: when citizens stop caring about public affairs, the state is lost. Mill, a century later, saw a new danger. A majority can be a tyrant too, and silencing even one person who disagrees harms everyone. The phrase "tyranny of the majority" comes from Alexis de Tocqueville; Mill made it famous.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

None of them trusts power without limits, whoever holds it. They split on whether ordinary people can be trusted to judge. Plato said no, Aristotle said often, and Mill said yes, but only with free speech protecting the few who disagree.

## Topic page: Freedom (society)

**Freedom** · theme: Freedom, choice, and intention, plus hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Are you free when nobody stops you, or when you master yourself?

### How the traditions see it

**The Stoics and the East: freedom inside.** Epictetus taught that some things are in our power and others are not; freedom starts with knowing the difference. Spinoza called being ruled by your emotions a kind of bondage. Confucius said an army can lose its general, but nobody can take away the will of an ordinary person. Here, freedom is something no prison can remove.

**Mill: freedom from others.** For Mill, the only freedom worth the name is to pursue your own good in your own way, as long as you do not stop others from doing the same. Over your own body and mind, you rule. Society may step in only to prevent harm to others. Yet Mill limited this to people "in the maturity of their faculties", and in the same chapter he called despotism legitimate over people he called "barbarians", if it improved them.

**Rousseau and Locke: freedom with others.** Rousseau opens *The Social Contract* by saying people are born free and everywhere in chains. His answer is not fewer laws, but laws the people make together. Locke agrees that law, done well, does not restrain freedom but protects and enlarges it.

**Wollstonecraft and Fuller: freedom for whom?** Both asked why freedom stopped at men. Wollstonecraft argued that blind obedience is what power wants, and education is the way out. Fuller wanted every path open to women as freely as to men.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

In 1958 the philosopher Isaiah Berlin named the split: "negative" freedom, being left alone, and "positive" freedom, being in control of your own life. Mill stands for the first, Rousseau for the second. Berlin warned that the second can be abused, when rulers claim to force people towards their "real" good. Liberals, in turn, would not call the Stoics' inner freedom real freedom at all.
