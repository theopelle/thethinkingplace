## Stoicism

### In one line

A practical philosophy for living well: train your judgments, act with virtue, and accept calmly what you cannot control.

### Where and when

Zeno of Citium founded the school in Athens around 300 BCE. He taught in the Stoa Poikile, a painted colonnade on the main square, which gave the school its name. Chrysippus, its third head, turned it into a full system of logic, physics, and ethics.

Stoicism then spread across the Greek and Roman world and lasted until the 3rd century CE. Its best-known voices are Roman: Seneca, a statesman; Epictetus, a teacher; and Marcus Aurelius, an emperor. It faded after Christianity became Rome's state religion, then came back in the Renaissance and again today.

### Key ideas

- **Virtue is the only good.** Health, wealth, and pleasure have value, but they do not make a life good or bad.
- **Some things are up to us, others are not.** Our judgments and actions are ours; most of the rest is not.
- **Live in agreement with nature,** which for the Stoics means living by reason.
- **Strong emotions come from false judgments,** so they can be changed by thinking more clearly.
- **Practice daily:** philosophy is a training, not a theory. Seneca, for one, looked back each evening at how he had spent his day.

### Who we read

Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, Seneca, and Boethius.

### Next to its neighbours

Stoics and Epicureans both wanted a calm mind, and both lived in the same city at the same time. They disagreed on the goal. For the Stoics it was virtue; for the Epicureans it was pleasure, understood as freedom from pain and fear. The Stoics also expected you to take part in public life, while the Epicureans mostly stayed out of politics.

### Where to start reading

Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*: private notes, written in short entries that you can open anywhere. Then Epictetus's *Enchiridion*, a short handbook.

## Epicureanism

### In one line

A philosophy of quiet happiness: simple pleasures, good friends, and a mind free from fear.

### Where and when

Epicurus founded the school in Athens in 307 BCE. It met in his garden, so it became known simply as "the Garden", and it welcomed women among its students. In the 1st century BCE, the Roman poet Lucretius set out its ideas in a long poem, *On the Nature of Things*.

The school was a rival first of Plato's followers, then of the Stoics. It declined as Christianity spread; Dante even placed the Epicureans in hell. Later thinkers rediscovered it, and Thomas Jefferson called himself an Epicurean.

### Key ideas

- **Pleasure is the goal of life,** but the pleasure that counts is the absence of pain in the body and of fear in the mind.
- **Desire less.** Some desires are natural and easy to meet; others, like the hunger for wealth and power, can never be satisfied.
- **Death is nothing to fear:** when it is here, we are not.
- **The world is made of atoms and empty space.** The gods, if they exist, do not interfere in human life.
- **Friendship** is the greatest help to a happy life.

### Who we read

Epicurus and Lucretius.

### Next to its neighbours

The word "epicurean" now suggests fine food and luxury. That is close to the opposite of what Epicurus taught: he and his friends were known for simple meals and long conversations. Next to the Stoics, the Epicureans also sought a calm mind, but through pleasure rather than virtue, and by keeping out of politics rather than serving the city.

### Where to start reading

Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus*: a few pages that hold the whole philosophy. Then the *Principal Doctrines*, a list of short sayings.

## Classical Greek Philosophy

### In one line

Where Western philosophy began: asking what the world is made of, and then how we should live.

### Where and when

Greek philosophy began in the 6th century BCE in the cities of the eastern Aegean, with thinkers who swapped myths for reasoned explanations. Around 500 BCE, Heraclitus of Ephesus saw the world as constant change.

In Athens, Socrates (about 470–399 BCE) turned philosophy towards ethics. He wrote nothing and was sentenced to death by an Athenian court. His student Plato founded the Academy, and Plato's student Aristotle founded the Lyceum and taught the young Alexander the Great. From there, a clear line runs to Roman, Islamic, medieval, Renaissance, and Enlightenment thought.

### Key ideas

- **Change is everywhere** (Heraclitus): "everything flows", yet a hidden order, the logos, holds it together.
- **Question everything,** including yourself (Socrates): his method of short questions and answers still bears his name.
- **Beyond what we see** (Plato): the changing world is an imperfect copy of perfect, unchanging Forms.
- **Character is built by practice** (Aristotle): we become good by doing good things, until they become habits.
- **The goal is flourishing** (*eudaimonia*): a whole life lived well, not a passing feeling.

### Who we read

Heraclitus, Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle.

### Next to its neighbours

The Stoics and the Epicureans grew out of this world, after Aristotle. Both took the classical question, how to live well, and turned it into a daily practice with a single clear answer: virtue for the Stoics, pleasure as calm for the Epicureans. The classical thinkers argued more and settled less. They also lived in a society built on slavery, and Aristotle argued that some people are slaves by nature.

### Where to start reading

Plato, *Apology*: Socrates' defence speech at his trial. It is short, and it shows why he chose death over silence.

## Enlightenment Ethics

### In one line

The age that put reason, evidence, and freedom at the centre of how we live and govern ourselves.

### Where and when

The Enlightenment grew out of the scientific revolution of Galileo and Newton. It took shape in Western Europe in the 17th century; one common starting point is Descartes' *Discourse on Method* (1637).

It peaked in the 18th century, in salons, coffeehouses, academies, and a booming world of print. Its great project was the French *Encyclopédie* (1751–1772), edited by Diderot and d'Alembert. Historians usually end the period with the French Revolution in 1789, or at the latest with Kant's death in 1804. Our path stretches a little further, to Mill in the 1860s.

### Key ideas

- **Think for yourself:** test beliefs by reason and evidence, not by tradition alone.
- **Why we need government** (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau): power rests on an agreement among the people, not on birth.
- **Freedom and tolerance:** of religion, of speech, of how you live.
- **Morality from feeling or from duty:** Hume and Adam Smith start from sympathy; Kant from duty; Mill from the greatest happiness.
- **Women's minds** (du Châtelet, Wollstonecraft): women are rational beings and deserve the same education.

### Who we read

Descartes, Hobbes, Spinoza, Locke, Hume, Voltaire, Émilie du Châtelet, Adam Smith, Rousseau, Diderot, Kant, Mary Wollstonecraft, and Mill.

### Next to its neighbours

The Humanists before them looked back to ancient wisdom; the Enlightenment looked forward, to progress. Its ideals also had clear limits. Many European colonies ran on slave labour, and even Locke, who called freedom a natural right, allowed slavery for captives taken in a just war. In 1791, enslaved people in the French colony of Saint-Domingue rose up, in what became the Haitian Revolution.

### Where to start reading

Mill, *On Liberty* (1859): the case for free thought and free living, still argued over today.

## Existential Thought

### In one line

Writers who faced life's hardest questions head-on: freedom, suffering, meaning, and how to live with them.

### Where and when

The word "existentialism" only appeared in the mid-20th century, with Sartre, de Beauvoir, and Camus. These best-known existentialists are still under copyright, so the app reads the writers who came before or alongside them.

The questions are older. Pascal, a French mathematician, asked them in the 1650s. In the 19th century, Schopenhauer, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, and Nietzsche pushed back against neat systems that explained everything except how to live. In America, William James asked what makes life worth living, and Simone Weil, who died in 1943, brings the path into the 20th century.

### Key ideas

- **Life comes before theory:** start from real human experience, not abstract systems.
- **Freedom is a gift and a weight.** We have to choose, and we carry the responsibility.
- **Anxiety is part of being free:** the dizziness of having to decide.
- **Meaning is not handed to us;** we have to find it, or make it.
- **Authenticity:** live your own life, not the one others expect.

### Who we read

Pascal, Schopenhauer, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Nietzsche, William James, and Simone Weil.

### Next to its neighbours

The Enlightenment trusted reason to improve the world. These writers doubted that reason alone could tell a person how to live. Some, like Pascal, Tolstoy, and Weil, answered through religious faith; others, like Schopenhauer and Nietzsche, did not. Nietzsche's work was later bent out of shape: after his death, his sister edited his writings to fit her nationalist views, and the Nazis claimed him, although he had openly opposed antisemitism and nationalism.

### Where to start reading

Dostoevsky, *Notes from Underground*: a short novel, angry and funny, about a man who refuses to be reasonable.

## Humanists and Essayists

### In one line

Writers who explored how to live in personal prose, short sayings, and poems, rather than in grand systems.

### Where and when

The path spans five centuries. It begins in Renaissance Italy in the 1340s with Petrarch, called the father of humanism, who collected and taught the ancient Greek and Roman writers. Humanism then spread north with printing, to Erasmus and beyond, while Machiavelli took a hard look at power as it is, not as it should be.

In France, Montaigne invented the essay in 1580, from the French *essayer*, "to try"; in England, Francis Bacon wrote the first essays in English in 1597. Then came the French moralists, La Rochefoucauld, La Bruyère, Vauvenargues, and Chamfort, masters of the short, sharp maxim.

From 1836, in New England, Emerson, Thoreau, and Margaret Fuller belonged to the Transcendentalist circle, which trusted intuition, nature, and self-reliance. Emily Dickinson and George Eliot share their spirit.

### Key ideas

- **Learn from the ancients,** then think for yourself.
- **Write from your own life:** Montaigne made himself the subject of his own book.
- **Say it short:** a maxim should surprise you into seeing something true.
- **Look at people as they are,** vanity and self-interest included.
- **Trust yourself and nature** over custom and crowds.

### Who we read

Petrarch, Erasmus, Machiavelli, Montaigne, Francis Bacon, La Rochefoucauld, La Bruyère, Vauvenargues, Chamfort, Emerson, Margaret Fuller, Thoreau, Emily Dickinson, and George Eliot.

### Next to its neighbours

The Stoics and Confucians tell you how to live. These writers mostly show you how they lived, or how people really behave, and let you draw the lesson. Some readers find the moralists cynical, and Machiavelli has given his name to ruthless politics. Read them as observers, not as role models.

### Where to start reading

Thoreau, *Walden*: two years in a cabin by a pond, and what he learned about needing less.

## Buddhist Philosophy

### In one line

The Buddha's teachings read as a practical philosophy: an ethics and a training of the mind to end suffering.

### Where and when

The Buddha, born Gautama in Lumbini, in today's Nepal, taught in northern India, most likely in the 5th century BCE. His teaching began as one of several movements of wandering seekers, then spread across Asia along the Silk Road, and reached the West in the 19th century.

Today it is both a religion, followed by hundreds of millions of people, and a philosophy studied by many who do not practise it. The app reads it mainly as philosophy, through two texts: the *Dhammapada*, a collection of 423 short verses in 26 chapters, and *The Questions of King Milinda*, a dialogue between a Buddhist monk and a Greek king of north-west India.

### Key ideas

- **Life is marked by *dukkha*:** suffering, or the unease of relying on things that do not last.
- **Everything changes,** yet we crave and cling to what cannot stay.
- **No fixed self:** there is no permanent, unchanging soul behind our thoughts and feelings.
- **Actions have consequences** (karma): what we do shapes who we become.
- **The Middle Way** between harsh self-denial and indulgence, through ethics, meditation, and wisdom.

### Who we read

The Buddha (the *Dhammapada*) and *The Questions of King Milinda*.

### Next to its neighbours

Buddhism grew in the same soil as Indian Philosophy and shares ideas such as karma and rebirth. But it parts ways on the self: where the Upanishads look for a deep, lasting self, Buddhism denies there is one. Its focus on calming the mind also echoes the Stoics and Epicureans, a few centuries later and a long way west.

### Where to start reading

The *Dhammapada*: short verses that can be read one at a time, in any order.

## Indian Philosophy

### In one line

Philosophy from India's Hindu tradition, asking what our deepest self is, and how to act well in the world.

### Where and when

It begins with the Upanishads, Sanskrit texts composed between about 800 and 300 BCE. They moved away from the rituals of the older Vedas towards meditation and questions about consciousness. The *Bhagavad Gita*, likely composed in the 2nd or 1st century BCE, is a 700-verse dialogue within the great epic, the *Mahabharata*.

Later, six classical schools took shape; one of them, Vedanta, became the most influential. In modern times, Swami Vivekananda brought Vedanta to the West, with a famous speech in Chicago in 1893. In 1913, the poet Rabindranath Tagore became the first non-European to win a Nobel Prize.

### Key ideas

- **Atman and Brahman:** the deepest self (Atman) is one with ultimate reality (Brahman), at least in the Vedanta school.
- **Act without clinging to results:** do your duty (*dharma*), but let go of the fruits of your actions.
- **Many paths:** through action, knowledge, and devotion.
- **Karma and rebirth:** actions shape what follows, in this life and beyond.
- **Liberation** (*moksha*): freedom from that cycle, through self-knowledge.

### Who we read

The Upanishads, the *Bhagavad Gita*, Vivekananda, and Tagore.

### Next to its neighbours

Buddhism shares karma and rebirth with this path but denies a lasting self, while the Upanishads put that self at the centre. The *Gita* also has a hard setting: on a battlefield, the god Krishna, in Hindu tradition, persuades the warrior Arjuna to fight. Readers have taken it very differently since. Gandhi learned verses from its second chapter by heart and treated them as a companion for his non-violent struggle.

### Where to start reading

The *Bhagavad Gita*: short chapters, each on one question about how to act.

## Taoism

### In one line

A Chinese tradition of living in harmony with the Tao, the natural way of things, through simplicity and effortless action.

### Where and when

Taoist thought took shape in China during the Warring States period, between about 450 and 300 BCE. Its two founding texts were compiled then: the *Tao Te Ching*, short poetic chapters traditionally credited to Laozi, and the *Zhuangzi*, full of stories.

Tradition makes Laozi an archivist at the Zhou court and an older contemporary of Confucius; scholars are divided, and some think he is partly or wholly legendary. Taoism later also became an organised religion, with its own priests, rituals, and scriptures. The *Tao Te Ching* is one of the most translated books in the world, second only to the Bible.

### Key ideas

- **The Tao** is the way of things: always changing, and impossible to fully put into words.
- **Effortless action** (*wu wei*): act without forcing, like water finding its way around a stone.
- **Naturalness** (*ziran*): let things be what they are.
- **Three treasures:** compassion, humility, and frugality.
- **Softness beats hardness:** the yielding outlasts the rigid.

### Who we read

Laozi and Zhuangzi.

### Next to its neighbours

Taoism and Confucianism grew up side by side and are often read as a pair. Confucians build a good society through learning, ritual, and duty. Taoists distrust that effort and prefer to step back: fewer rules, less striving, more trust in nature. Both shaped Chinese life for over two thousand years, and many people drew on both.

### Where to start reading

Laozi, *Tao Te Ching*: 81 short chapters, best read slowly, one at a time.

## Confucianism

### In one line

A Chinese tradition built on good character, learning, and harmony in families and society.

### Where and when

Confucius (about 551–479 BCE) was born near Qufu, in the small state of Lu, in eastern China. He saw himself not as an inventor but as a transmitter of older values that his age had lost. He taught thousands of students, and his sayings were collected by his followers in the *Analects*, many years after his death.

Mencius developed his ideas a century later. Suppressed under the harsh Qin dynasty, Confucianism became China's official philosophy under the Han, around 130 BCE. For centuries it was the basis of the exams that chose government officials, until they were abolished in 1905.

### Key ideas

- **Humaneness** (*ren*): kindness and care for others, the heart of a good person.
- **Ritual and good manners** (*li*): the forms that make respect visible, from greetings to funerals.
- **The "superior person"** (*junzi*): noble by character, not by birth.
- **Family duty:** respect for parents, the root of a good society.
- **Lead by example:** a ruler who acts rightly will be followed without force.

### Who we read

Confucius and his students, and Mencius.

### Next to its neighbours

Where Taoists step back from society, Confucians step into it, and try to make it better through learning and duty. The tradition also built rules that bound women. From the Han period on, Confucian teachers taught that a virtuous woman should follow her father, then her husband, then her sons. Early 20th-century Chinese reformers blamed Confucianism for China's troubles, but interest in it has returned since.

### Where to start reading

Confucius, *Analects*: short sayings and exchanges with his students. Open it anywhere.

## Japanese Aesthetics

### In one line

Finding beauty in what is simple, imperfect, and passing, and paying full attention to small moments.

### Where and when

The path covers nine centuries of Japanese writing. Around 1000 CE, Sei Shōnagon, a lady at the imperial court in Kyoto, wrote *The Pillow Book*: lists, scenes, and sharp opinions about what is beautiful and what is not.

In 1212, Kamo no Chōmei, a court poet who had lived through fires, famine, and an earthquake in Kyoto, left society for a tiny hut in the mountains and wrote *An Account of a Ten-Foot-Square Hut*. In the early 14th century, the Buddhist monk Kenkō wrote *Essays in Idleness*, still one of the most studied works of medieval Japanese literature.

In 1906, Okakura Kakuzō wrote *The Book of Tea* in English, to explain Japanese culture to Western readers who often caricatured it.

### Key ideas

- **Mono no aware,** "the pathos of things": a gentle sadness at how everything passes, which makes it more beautiful.
- **Wabi:** the beauty of what is plain and stark.
- **Sabi:** the beauty of age and wear, like moss on stone.
- **Yūgen:** deep, quiet grace that is felt more than seen.
- **Simplicity** in daily life, as in the tea ceremony.

### Who we read

Sei Shōnagon, Kamo no Chōmei, Kenkō, and Kakuzo Okakura.

### Next to its neighbours

These ideals grew from Shinto's love of nature and Buddhism's teaching that all things are impermanent, with Taoist and Zen influences as well. Where the Buddhists see impermanence as a source of suffering to overcome, these writers often find it a source of beauty to enjoy.

### Where to start reading

Okakura, *The Book of Tea*: short, written in English, and a door into the whole path.

## Persian and Sufi Poetry

### In one line

Wisdom poetry from the Persian-speaking world and from Sufism, on love, longing, the present moment, and how to treat one another.

### Where and when

The path runs from the 11th to the 14th century, across Persia and the wider Islamic world. It opens with Omar Khayyam (1048–1131) of Nishapur, a mathematician and astronomer who designed a calendar still at the root of the Persian one; short four-line poems attributed to him question fate and celebrate the present. Al-Ghazali (about 1058–1111), a leading scholar in Baghdad, gave up his post after a personal crisis in 1095 and turned to the Sufi path.

Ibn Arabi (1165–1240), born in Murcia, in today's Spain, taught that all things are expressions of one single reality. Rumi (1207–1273) settled in Konya, in today's Turkey; his long poem, the *Masnavi*, is among the greatest in Persian. Sa'di (about 1210–1291) and Hafiz (about 1325–1390) both came from Shiraz, and many Persian speakers still know Hafiz's poems by heart.

### Key ideas

- **Love** as the deepest force in a life, often love of God, sometimes of a person, and often both at once.
- **Seize the day** (Khayyam): life is short and uncertain.
- **We are one family:** Sa'di's lines on all people as parts of one body hang on a carpet in the United Nations building.
- **Teach through stories,** as in Sa'di's *Gulistan*.
- **The inner path:** purify the heart and let go of the ego.

### Who we read

Omar Khayyam, Sa'di, Rumi, Hafiz, al-Ghazali, and Ibn Arabi.

### Next to its neighbours

Sufism is the spiritual and mystical tradition within Islam, centred on love of God and remembrance of Him. Khayyam was not a Sufi, which is why the path has its wider name. In the West, these poets are often read through free translations: Edward FitzGerald's 1859 version of Khayyam made him famous in English, but it is free, close to a paraphrase in places.

### Where to start reading

Sa'di, *Gulistan* (*The Rose Garden*): short stories with a lesson, in prose and verse.
