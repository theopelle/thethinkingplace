## Topic page: The self and the soul

**The self and the soul** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Is there a lasting "you" behind your body, your thoughts, and your moods?

### How the traditions see it

**The Upanishads (Indian Philosophy).** The Upanishads look for the *Atman*, the deepest self, behind body and mind. It is what makes all the other faculties work. Their boldest claim is that this inner self is one with *Brahman*, the reality behind the whole universe. The Katha Upanishad pictures it as a traveller sitting in a chariot: the body is the chariot, and the intellect holds the reins.

**The Buddhist texts (Buddhist Philosophy).** Buddhism turned this around. Its teaching of *anatta*, "not-self", says there is no permanent soul at all. A person is made of five changing parts: body, feelings, perceptions, impulses, and consciousness. In *The Questions of King Milinda*, the monk Nagasena uses the same image of a chariot: it is only a name for its parts working together, and so is a person.

**Plato (Classical Greek Philosophy).** For Plato, the soul is the true self. It is not made of matter, and it does not die with the body. In the *Phaedrus* he uses a chariot too: reason is the driver, pulled by two horses, one noble and one ruled by appetite. A good life is a driver who keeps both in hand.

**Al-Ghazali and the Sufis (Persian and Sufi Poetry).** In Islam the word *nafs* means self, but for the Sufis it is above all the lower self, the ego with its pride and cravings. The Quran names three stages: the self that urges us to do wrong, the self that blames itself, and the self at peace. Al-Ghazali compared the body to a horse and the soul to its rider, and called the struggle with the lower self the greatest struggle of all.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Three of these traditions reach for the same picture, a chariot, which says something about the question: who, if anyone, is holding the reins? The Upanishads and Plato answer with a lasting self that outlives the body. The Buddhist texts answer that there is no one there to find, a view Hume reached by another road when he called the self a bundle of perceptions. The Sufis care less about what the self is than about what to do with it. And Epicurus disagreed with almost everyone: for him, the soul ends with the body, so death is nothing to fear.

## Topic page: Karma and dharma

**Karma and dharma** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Do our actions shape who we become, and is there a right path for each of us?

### How the traditions see it

**The Upanishads (Indian Philosophy).** *Karma* simply means "action". In the older Vedic religion, it meant ritual acts that could earn a place in heaven. The Upanishads made it inward. In one of them, a person is made of desires: as we desire, so we will, and as we will, so we act. What we do, in turn, shapes what we become, in this life and the next.

**The Bhagavad Gita (Indian Philosophy).** *Dharma* comes from a root meaning "to hold" or "to support": the order that holds life together, and the right way to live within it. The Gita adds *svadharma*, your own dharma, and says it is better to follow your own path, even badly, than someone else's well. Its answer to karma is to act fully, but without craving the results. *Yoga*, in the Gita, is that discipline of action.

**The Buddhist texts (Buddhist Philosophy).** The Buddha moved karma from the act to the mind behind it: "Intention, I tell you, is karma." It is not fate: past actions shape what happens to us, but not how we respond. In Buddhism, *Dhamma* (the Pali form of dharma) also means something else: the Buddha's teaching itself, and the truth it points to. When the *Dhammapada* speaks of "the law", this is what it means.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Both traditions agree that actions have consequences that reach beyond the moment, and that what we do slowly makes us who we are. They part on the self: the Upanishads have a lasting self that carries its karma, while Buddhism has no permanent self at all. There is also an uncomfortable side. In Hindu law, each person's own dharma was tied to their class and station in life. Early Buddhist texts pushed back: "Not by birth is one a brahmin or an outcaste, but by deeds."

## Topic page: Duty

**Duty** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Should we do what is right even when we don't feel like it, and even when it costs us?

### How the traditions see it

**Kant (Enlightenment Ethics).** For Kant, nothing is good without condition except a good will. An act has moral worth only when it is done from duty, out of respect for the moral law, not from feeling or for the results. That law comes from reason itself, and it commands without conditions: Kant calls it the categorical imperative. Because every person has this reason, every person has equal worth and deserves equal respect.

**The Stoics (Stoicism).** The Stoics spoke of *kathekon*, the fitting or proper action: what our nature and our roles call for, as parents, citizens, or friends. Cicero's *On Duties*, one of the most read books of Roman ethics, is built on this idea. Marcus Aurelius put it shortly: stop talking about what a good person should be, and be one.

**Confucius and Mencius (Confucianism).** In Confucian ethics, duty lives in relationships: to parents, rulers, friends, and strangers. Righteousness, *yi*, is one of the five qualities of the gentleman, beside benevolence, ritual, wisdom, and trustworthiness. Mencius said a person should simply not do what their own sense of righteousness forbids.

**The Bhagavad Gita (Indian Philosophy).** The Gita speaks of *svadharma*, your own duty, set by your nature and place in life. Arjuna must fight, because that is his duty as a warrior, but without craving the fruits of his action. (See also *Karma and dharma*.)

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them say duty is not just a feeling we wait for. They disagree on where it comes from. For Kant, it comes from reason, the same for everyone. For the Stoics and Confucius, it comes from our nature and our roles. For the Gita, it comes from our station, which in its world was tied to birth. Mill, by contrast, saw the inner pull of duty as a feeling in our own mind, trained by upbringing. And in 1943 Simone Weil argued that duties come before rights: a right means something only when others are bound to respect it.

## Topic page: Fortune

**Fortune** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

When luck turns against us, should we accept it, or fight it?

### How the traditions see it

**Epictetus and Seneca (Stoicism).** Epictetus began his handbook with one division: some things are in our power, and others are not. Our judgements, choices, and desires are ours; health, wealth, and reputation are not. Seneca drew the lesson: fortune can take away only what she gave.

**Boethius (Stoicism).** Boethius was a senior Roman official who lost everything and wrote in prison, waiting for death. In his *Consolation*, Philosophy herself visits him. She shows him that riches, status, power, and pleasure are only the goods of fortune, of little real worth, and that true happiness lies in virtue, which fortune cannot touch. Fortune turns her wheel, and complaining that it turns is foolish.

**Descartes (Enlightenment Ethics).** Descartes made the Stoic answer a rule for his own life: to try always to master himself rather than fortune, and to change his desires rather than the order of the world.

**Machiavelli (Humanists and Essayists).** Machiavelli saw *Fortuna* as the great enemy of political order. In *The Prince* he compares her to a river in flood that sweeps everything away. Yet floods can be prepared for: precautions taken in calm weather can turn aside the worst of the damage, and a ruler's skill, his *virtù*, lies in that preparation. In the same chapter he also wrote that fortune is a woman who must be beaten and mastered, an image that shocks readers today.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

They all agree that much of life is out of our hands. They disagree on what to do about it. The Stoics, Boethius, and Descartes turn inward: change your desires, and fortune loses its hold. Machiavelli turns outward: prepare, act boldly, and win back some of what luck would take. Buddhism offers a third view: what happens to us may follow from past actions, but how we respond is still ours (see *Karma and dharma*).
