## Michel de Montaigne

**Michel de Montaigne** · Saint-Michel-de-Montaigne, France · 1533 – 1592 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Montaigne grew up on his family's estate near Bordeaux, in a wealthy family whose fortune began with a herring merchant. His father had an unusual plan for him: Latin was to be his first language, taught by a German tutor who spoke no French.

He trained in law and worked as a magistrate in Bordeaux, where he met his great friend Étienne de La Boétie. In 1571, on his 38th birthday, he withdrew to a tower of his château, with a library of some 1,500 books. There he began the *Essays*, first published in 1580, and kept adding to them until his death.

### What he cared about

Montaigne's subject was himself, as an example of being human. His big themes:

- **What do I know?** His famous question: doubt, especially about our own certainties.
- **Learning to die** is learning to be free.
- **Living well** is about how we use our time, not how long we live.
- **Other cultures:** we call barbarous whatever is not our own custom.
- **Honesty:** to be the same inside as outside.

### What might surprise you

He wrote in the middle of France's brutal wars between Catholics and Protestants. A Catholic himself, he was trusted by both sides, and acted as a moderating voice between the Catholic king and the Protestant Henry of Navarre.

From 1578 he suffered from painful kidney stones, and still avoided doctors and drugs. His *Essays*, translated into English in 1603, very likely influenced Shakespeare: a scene in *The Tempest* closely follows one of them.

### Where to start reading

"That to Study Philosophy Is to Learn to Die" or "Of Cannibals", two short essays from Book I. Charles Cotton's translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Baruch Spinoza

**Baruch Spinoza** · Amsterdam, Netherlands · 1632 – 1677 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Spinoza was born in Amsterdam into a family of Portuguese Jews who had fled to the more tolerant Dutch Republic. He received a traditional Jewish education. In 1656, at 23, the leaders of his community expelled him for his views, with the harshest ban they had ever pronounced.

From then on he belonged to no religious group. He earned his living by grinding lenses and lived quietly in Rijnsburg, Voorburg, and The Hague, surrounded by a small circle of friends. He turned down a chair of philosophy at Heidelberg, perhaps to keep his freedom of thought, and died at 44.

### What he cared about

In his *Ethics*, set out like a geometry book, with definitions and proofs, he looks for a calm, free life:

- **God or Nature:** there is only one reality, and it can be called either.
- **Bondage** is being ruled by our emotions; **freedom** is understanding them.
- **Understanding heals:** an emotion loses its grip once we see it clearly.
- **The free person thinks of life,** not of death.
- **Happiness is not the reward of virtue,** but virtue itself.

### What might surprise you

His *Theological-Political Treatise* questioned the divine origin of the Bible and argued that religious authorities should have no role in a democratic state. It was banned as blasphemous, and his *Ethics* was published only after his death, by friends.

The lens grinding that fed him may also have helped kill him: he died of a lung disease, probably tuberculosis, possibly made worse by glass dust. Einstein later named him as the philosopher who most shaped his view of the world.

### Where to start reading

The *Ethics*, Parts IV and V, on human bondage and freedom. Hard going, but worth it. R. H. M. Elwes's 1883 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Mencius

**Mencius** · Zoucheng, China · about 371 – 289 BCE · Confucianism

### Who he was

Mencius, born Meng Ke, came from the small state of Zou, in eastern China, about a century after Confucius. He lost his father young, and his mother, who raised him, became a model of wise parenting in Chinese tradition. He is said to have studied in the school of Confucius's grandson, Zisi.

Like Confucius, he spent years travelling from court to court, advising rulers during the violent Warring States period. He served for a time as an official and scholar in the state of Qi, and left to mourn his mother for three years. Disappointed that rulers ignored his advice, he finally withdrew from public life.

### What he cared about

The book named after him, the *Mencius*, records his conversations with rulers and students. His central claim: human nature is good.

- **Anyone who sees a child about to fall into a well** feels alarm at once, without thinking of reward. That is the seed of goodness in all of us.
- **Four beginnings:** compassion, shame, respect, and a sense of right and wrong, which we must grow like plants.
- **Shame matters:** a person without it cannot become good.
- **Rulers serve the people:** a state that governs humanely will flourish.

### What might surprise you

The gentle optimist was also a political radical for his time. He argued that people may overthrow, or even kill, a ruler who ignores their needs and rules harshly, because such a ruler is no longer a true ruler.

His view of human nature set him against another Confucian, Xunzi, who thought people are born bad. Centuries later, his book became one of the Four Books at the core of Confucian education, and he is honoured as the "Second Sage".

### Where to start reading

The *Mencius*, Book II, Part I, with the child at the well. James Legge's 1895 translation is free online.

## Swami Vivekananda

**Swami Vivekananda** · Calcutta, India · 1863 – 1902 · Indian Philosophy

### Who he was

Vivekananda was born Narendranath Datta into a well-off family in Calcutta, the son of a lawyer. As a young man he was drawn to a reform movement that rejected caste barriers. At eighteen he met the mystic Ramakrishna, became his closest disciple, and later took the vows of a monk.

After Ramakrishna's death he spent five years walking across India, living on alms, and saw for himself the poverty of millions under British rule. In 1893 he sailed to Chicago for the Parliament of the World's Religions. Back in India, he founded the Ramakrishna Mission in 1897, devoted to education and social service. He died in 1902, aged 39.

### What he cared about

The app reads his *Karma-Yoga* (1896), lectures on the path of action:

- **Work without attachment:** act fully, but do not cling to the results.
- **Misery comes from attachment,** not from work itself.
- **Do good because it is good,** not for money, fame, or reward.
- **Giving blesses the giver:** in helping the world, we help ourselves.
- **Teach yourself:** no one is ever really taught by another.

### What might surprise you

His Chicago speech, which opened with the words "Sisters and brothers of America", called for religious tolerance and is still remembered today. He went on to lecture in the United States and England, and two American universities offered him academic posts.

He was a monk, yet he urged practical action: schools, relief work, and service to the poor. Today his birthday, 12 January, is celebrated in India as National Youth Day.

### Where to start reading

*Karma-Yoga*, chapter 3, "The Secret of Work". Short lectures in plain English, free online.

## John Stuart Mill

**John Stuart Mill** · London, England · 1806 – 1873 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Mill was born in London, the eldest son of the philosopher James Mill, who educated him at home with help from Jeremy Bentham. He learned Greek at three, and by eight had read the whole of the historian Herodotus. From the age of 17, he worked for 35 years for the East India Company, as his father had.

At twenty, the strain caught up with him: he went through months of deep sadness, and the poetry of Wordsworth helped him find joy again. In 1851 he married Harriet Taylor, his close friend of twenty years, whose ideas shaped his work. Later he served as a Member of Parliament, and he died in Avignon, where she is buried, in 1873.

### What he cared about

Mill is often called the most influential English-speaking philosopher of the 19th century. His central themes:

- **Liberty:** you may pursue your own good in your own way, as long as you do not harm others.
- **Free speech:** silencing even one dissenting voice robs everyone, since we learn by hearing the other side.
- **Individuality:** human nature is not a machine but a tree, which needs room to grow.
- **Happiness,** but of a higher kind: better a dissatisfied human than a satisfied pig.
- **Women's equality,** argued in *The Subjection of Women* (1869).

### What might surprise you

As an MP, he tried to change the Reform Bill to give the vote to every "person" instead of every "man". He was only the second member of Parliament to call for women's suffrage. He was also the godfather of the philosopher Bertrand Russell.

His defence of liberty had limits, though. In *On Liberty* he called despotism a legitimate way to govern people he called "barbarians", provided it improved them, a view tied to the empire he served for most of his career.

### Where to start reading

*On Liberty* (1859), chapters I to III. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## Kakuzo Okakura

**Kakuzo Okakura** · Yokohama, Japan · 1863 – 1913 · Japanese Aesthetics

### Who he was

Okakura grew up in Yokohama, studying Western culture at school and Japanese tradition in a Buddhist temple. At the Tokyo Imperial University he studied with the American art historian Ernest Fenollosa, and he went on to work for the Ministry of Education.

In 1887 he helped found the Tokyo School of Fine Arts, and he soon became its director. When it began to favour European methods, he resigned and started a new academy with leading artists, to learn from Western art without losing Japanese inspiration. From 1906 he worked at the Museum of Fine Arts in Boston, where he became curator of Japanese and Chinese art.

### What he cared about

He wrote his main works in English, to speak directly to Western readers. In *The Book of Tea* (1906), tea becomes a way of seeing life:

- **The beauty of the imperfect:** true beauty is found by one who completes the incomplete in the mind.
- **Emptiness is useful:** a pitcher's value lies in the space where water goes.
- **Leave something unsaid,** so others can finish the thought.
- **Small things reveal us:** the art of life lies in everyday details.
- **East and West can complete each other,** if each learns to respect the other.

### What might surprise you

*The Book of Tea* was partly a reply. Written just after the Russo-Japanese War, it protested Western caricatures of Japan, and the fear that Japan was respected only once it adopted the West's taste for war.

He also spent two years in India and talked with Vivekananda and Tagore, two other voices in the app. In Boston, he once hosted a tea ceremony by candlelight for the art collector Isabella Stewart Gardner.

### Where to start reading

*The Book of Tea*, chapter I. Short, warm, and written in English. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## Omar Khayyam

**Omar Khayyam** · Nishapur, Iran · 1048 – 1131 · Persian and Sufi Poetry

### Who he was

Omar Khayyam was born in Nishapur, in north-east Persia, and lived under the Seljuk sultans, around the time of the First Crusade. He was first of all a scientist. In mathematics, he found a general way to solve cubic equations using geometry, centuries before Descartes.

In 1074 the sultan Malik-Shah called him to his service and asked him to set up an observatory in Isfahan. There Khayyam measured the length of the solar year with great precision and designed a new calendar, which is still the basis of the Persian calendar today.

### What he cared about

The short four-line poems, or quatrains, attributed to him are full of questions, doubt, and a love of the present:

- **Time cannot be undone:** the moving finger writes, and nothing will cancel a line.
- **We cannot see behind the veil:** the great questions stay without answers.
- **Learned arguments go round in circles,** and we come out by the same door we went in.
- **Enjoy the present:** a book of verses, bread, wine, and good company.
- **Everything passes,** sultans and their glory included.

### What might surprise you

We are not sure he wrote the poems at all. No collection survives from his lifetime, and some scholars think the whole tradition of "Khayyam's quatrains" may have been attached to his name later.

In his own time, his doubts made religious leaders suspicious, and after he fell from favour at court he made the pilgrimage to Mecca, perhaps partly to prove his faith. In the West he became famous through Edward FitzGerald's free English version of 1859, which the Pre-Raphaelites came to admire.

### Where to start reading

FitzGerald's *Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám*, first edition (1859): 75 quatrains, read in ten minutes, and free on Project Gutenberg. Remember it is FitzGerald's poem as much as Khayyam's.

## William James

**William James** · New York, United States · 1842 – 1910 · Existential Thought

### Who he was

James was born in New York into a wealthy, unusual family: his father was a religious thinker, his brother Henry became a great novelist, and his sister Alice a diarist. He trained as a doctor at Harvard but never practised. In 1865 he joined a scientific expedition up the Amazon.

He went on to teach at Harvard, where he gave the first psychology course in the United States, and is often called the father of American psychology. With Charles Sanders Peirce, he also founded the philosophy known as pragmatism. Students remembered him for his kindness and lack of arrogance.

### What he cared about

James asked what difference an idea makes in real life. His themes:

- **Is life worth living?** Yes, if we are willing to act and take risks without guarantees.
- **Need and struggle** bring us alive; triumph often leaves a void.
- **The right to believe:** on some questions, we must choose before all the evidence is in.
- **Ideals and "over-beliefs"** are the most interesting thing about a person.
- **The stream of consciousness:** the mind flows, it does not come in neat pieces.

### What might surprise you

The philosopher of hope knew despair from the inside. As a young man he went through long periods of depression and poor health. In 1870, reading the French philosopher Charles Renouvier, he decided to believe in his own free will, and he later called that day a crisis in his life.

His idea of a "stream of consciousness" travelled far beyond psychology: it shaped modernist writers such as James Joyce.

### Where to start reading

"Is Life Worth Living?", an essay in *The Will to Believe* (1897). Short, direct, and free online.

## Simone Weil

**Simone Weil** · Paris, France · 1909 – 1943 · Existential Thought

### Who she was

Weil was born in Paris into an agnostic Jewish family from Alsace; her elder brother André became a famous mathematician. At school her radical opinions earned her the nickname "the Red Virgin". She became a philosophy teacher, taking breaks for poor health and for political work.

She wanted to know the lives of working people from the inside. She took a year off teaching to work, unknown, on factory lines, including at Renault. In 1936 she joined anarchist fighters in the Spanish Civil War, until she badly burned herself in a pot of boiling cooking oil and had to leave.

### What she cared about

Weil wrote about suffering, justice, and attention, and she grew more and more drawn to Christian mysticism. In the essay the app reads, on the right use of school studies, she argues:

- **Attention** is the rarest and purest form of generosity.
- **Those who suffer** need, above all, someone able to pay attention to them.
- **No real effort of attention is ever lost,** even when it seems to fail.
- **Intelligence grows in joy,** and is led by desire, not by force.

### What might surprise you

She was drawn to Christianity yet long refused baptism, believing that such a step had to come from God, not from her own decision. She may have been baptized only in her last months.

During the Second World War she worked for the Free French in London. Ill with tuberculosis, she refused to eat more than people in occupied France, and died of heart failure in 1943, at 34. Almost all her books were published after her death, and later inspired writers from Albert Camus to Iris Murdoch.

### Where to start reading

"Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God": a short essay on attention. The app uses its own translation of the French.

## Henry David Thoreau

**Henry David Thoreau** · Concord, United States · 1817 – 1862 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Thoreau was born in Concord, Massachusetts, the son of a pencil maker, and studied at Harvard. Back home, he became part of the circle around Ralph Waldo Emerson, the leading Transcendentalist. He earned his living in many small ways, including as a land surveyor, and filled his notebooks with close observations of nature.

In 1845 he built a small cabin on land Emerson owned, by Walden Pond, and lived there for two years and two months. He died of tuberculosis in 1862, aged 44.

### What he cared about

*Walden* (1854) turns those years in the woods into one year, season by season. Its central ideas:

- **Live deliberately:** face only the essential facts of life, and see what they teach.
- **Simplify, simplify:** our lives are frittered away by detail.
- **Quiet desperation:** most people, he thought, are stuck in lives they never chose.
- **Your own drummer:** if you keep a different pace, perhaps you hear different music.
- **Resist injustice:** a citizen may refuse to obey an unjust state.

### What might surprise you

In 1846 he refused to pay a tax, in protest against slavery and the war with Mexico, and spent a night in jail. Someone, probably his aunt, paid it against his wishes. The night led to his essay "Civil Disobedience", which later inspired Tolstoy, Gandhi, and Martin Luther King Jr.

He was also an active abolitionist and helped people escaping slavery on the Underground Railroad. And his life at Walden was less cut off than the book suggests: he sometimes took his laundry home to Concord to be washed.

### Where to start reading

*Walden*, the chapter "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For". Free on Project Gutenberg.
