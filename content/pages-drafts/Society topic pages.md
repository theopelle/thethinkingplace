## Topic page: Equality (society)

**Equality** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Are people equal, and equal in what?

### How the traditions see it

**Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy).** Aristotle gave the basic rule of fairness: treat like cases alike. Citizens, for him, took turns ruling and being ruled. But he did not think all people were alike: in the same book he argued that some are slaves by nature, an idea later used to defend slavery.

**The Stoics and Confucius (Stoicism, Confucianism).** Other traditions stressed what we share. The Stoics taught the natural equality of all beings who have reason. Confucius said people are much alike by nature, and grow apart only by practice.

**Rousseau (Enlightenment Ethics).** Rousseau opens *The Social Contract* by saying people are born free, yet everywhere they are in chains. In his earlier *Discourse on Inequality*, he traced social inequality to the first fences around land: we are lost, he warned, if we forget that the fruits belong to all and the earth to no one.

**Wollstonecraft and Fuller (Enlightenment Ethics, Humanists and Essayists).** Both asked why equality stopped at men. Wollstonecraft argued that women seemed weaker only because they were denied education. Fuller noted that the self-reliance praised in her was called a fault in most women.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Until the 18th century, most thinkers assumed people were unequal by nature; the idea of natural rights changed that. Today most agree people are equal in dignity. The open question is equal in what: rights, chances, or results. Rousseau worried about unequal property; Aristotle, even with his blind spots, about treating like cases alike.

## Topic page: Tolerance (society)

**Tolerance** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

How do we live with people who believe differently?

### How the traditions see it

**Locke (Enlightenment Ethics).** In *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689), Locke argued that faith cannot be forced, so the state should not try. Churches are voluntary groups, and religion is a matter for each person. But his tolerance had limits: he refused it to atheists, whose promises he thought could not be trusted, and to churches whose members owed loyalty to a foreign ruler.

**Voltaire (Enlightenment Ethics).** After a Protestant merchant, Jean Calas, was executed in Toulouse in 1762 on a false charge, Voltaire wrote his *Treatise on Tolerance*. In his *Philosophical Dictionary* he calls tolerance the mark of our humanity: we are all made of weakness and error, so let us forgive each other's foolishness. Prejudice, he adds, is an opinion without judgement, taught to children before they can think.

**Mill (Enlightenment Ethics).** Mill moved the question from religion to every kind of opinion. If all of humanity but one person agreed, they would have no more right to silence that person than the one would have to silence them. Silencing a view, he said, assumes we cannot be wrong.

**Ibn Arabi and Okakura (Persian and Sufi Poetry, Japanese Aesthetics).** Ibn Arabi wrote of a heart that can take on every form: a monastery, a temple, a place of pilgrimage. Okakura, writing in English for Western readers, said East and West had grown along different lines, with no reason why one should not complete the other.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them see that forcing belief does not work and that our own views may be wrong. They disagree on the limits. Locke still excluded some groups; Voltaire mostly had religious violence in mind; Mill defended almost any opinion, short of harm to others.

## Topic page: Work and wealth (society)

**Work and wealth** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

How much is enough, and what is work for?

### How the traditions see it

**Epicurus and Marcus Aurelius (Epicureanism, Stoicism).** Epicurus said nature's wealth is limited and easy to get, while the wealth of empty wishes has no end. Marcus Aurelius, emperor of Rome, reminded himself each morning that he was rising to do the work of a human being.

**Confucius and Sa'di (Confucianism, Persian and Sufi Poetry).** Confucius could find joy with coarse rice and water, and called riches gained by wrongdoing a floating cloud. Being poor without complaining is hard, he said; being rich without pride is easy. Sa'di advised anyone who wants riches to ask for contentment instead.

**Adam Smith and Rousseau (Enlightenment Ethics).** Adam Smith argued in *The Wealth of Nations* that wealth grows through free exchange and the division of labour, as if guided by an "invisible hand". Yet his first book began with sympathy: however selfish we are, we care about others' happiness. Rousseau saw a darker side: the first person who fenced off land, said "This is mine", and found people simple enough to believe it, opened the way to crimes, wars, and misery.

**Vivekananda and Schopenhauer (Indian Philosophy, Existential Thought).** Vivekananda taught that the best work is done for no reward, neither money nor fame. Schopenhauer compared riches to sea water: the more you drink, the thirstier you get.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Most of them warn that wealth beyond need rarely brings peace. They differ on what wealth does for society. Smith saw trade and work as the way whole nations grow richer; Rousseau saw property as the root of inequality; the Stoics and the *Gita* tradition care less about the wealth than about the spirit in which we work.

## Topic page: War and peace (society)

**War and peace** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Can war ever be right, and what makes peace last?

### How the traditions see it

**Laozi and the Dhammapada (Taoism, Buddhist Philosophy).** The *Tao Te Ching* says the most skilled warrior shows no warlike look and never gives way to rage. The *Dhammapada* sees what victory costs: it breeds hatred, because the loser is unhappy. And the greatest victory is over yourself, not over a thousand armies.

**The Bhagavad Gita (Indian Philosophy).** The *Gita* is set on a battlefield. Prince Arjuna sees relatives, friends, and teachers in the enemy ranks, drops his bow, and refuses to fight. Krishna urges him to do his duty as a warrior. Yet Gandhi learned the last verses of its second chapter by heart and kept them as a companion in his non-violent movement.

**Hobbes and Kant (Enlightenment Ethics).** Hobbes, who lived through civil war, described life without government as a war of all against all. Peace, for him, needs a strong ruler; a commonwealth, he wrote, is the mother of peace. Kant hoped for more: in *Towards Perpetual Peace* (1795) he proposed republics joined in a union of states.

**Voltaire and Tolstoy (Enlightenment Ethics, Existential Thought).** Voltaire noted a sad truth: wishing your country to be great usually means wishing harm on your neighbours. Tolstoy went furthest: read literally, he said, the Sermon on the Mount forbids resisting evil by force. His ideas on non-violence deeply impressed Gandhi.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

None of them see war as glorious in itself. They split on whether it can be justified. The *Gita* accepts a just duty to fight, and Hobbes and Kant seek peace through political order; Tolstoy rejects violence altogether. Sa'di adds a quieter thought: a falsehood that brings peace can be better than a truth that stirs up harm.

## Topic page: Law and rights (society)

**Law and rights** · hand-picked thoughts · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Where do laws get their authority, and what rights do we have?

### How the traditions see it

**Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy).** Is it better to be ruled by the best person or the best laws? Aristotle leaned towards laws, because even good rulers have passions that bend their judgement. The law, he said, is reason unaffected by desire. Without law and justice, the human being is the worst of animals.

**Confucius (Confucianism).** Confucius trusted example more than rules. To govern means to correct, and a ruler who acts rightly is followed without giving orders. When wronged, repay injury with justice, and kindness with kindness.

**Epicurus (Epicureanism).** Epicurus saw no justice written into nature. Justice is an agreement between people not to harm each other or be harmed, and it can differ from place to place and time to time.

**Locke, Rousseau, and Wollstonecraft (Enlightenment Ethics).** Locke argued that all people have natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and that good law does not shrink freedom but protects and enlarges it. Rousseau wanted laws the people make together, and warned that a state is lost once citizens stop caring. Wollstonecraft demanded that rights include women.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Most of them see good law as a defence against the passions of the powerful; Sa'di put it simply: a tyrant cannot govern, for no one expects the shepherd's duty from the wolf. They split on where law comes from. For Aristotle and Locke, it rests on reason and nature; for Epicurus, on agreement; for Confucius, it matters less than the ruler's character.
