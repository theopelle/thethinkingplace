## Hafiz

**Hafiz** · Shiraz, Iran · about 1325 – 1390 · Persian and Sufi Poetry

### Who he was

Hafiz was born in Shiraz, in what is now Iran, and spent nearly all his life there. We know few facts about him: even his birth year is uncertain, and most stories about his youth are later legends.

As a young man he learned the Quran by heart. "Hafiz", the title for someone who has done so, became his pen name. He was a Sufi, and he lived on the patronage of a series of rulers in Shiraz, some tolerant and some strict. Around 1368 he fell out of favour at court and only regained his place some 20 years later.

His fame spread in his lifetime. He exchanged letters and poems with the Sultan of Bengal. His poems were gathered into the *Divan*, probably after his death in 1390.

### What he cared about

He wrote ghazals: short lyrics of love, wine, and longing that can be read as earthly love, love of God, or both. His themes:

- **Love is a hard road:** it looks easy from outside and asks a great deal from within.
- **Life is short:** our days are few, so meet them fully, like a rose that blooms without complaint.
- **Power passes:** the might of rulers lasts an hour, then nothing of it remains.
- **Against hypocrisy:** he exposes it wherever he finds it.
- **Mystery:** some gates stay locked to human wisdom, and he accepts that.

### What might surprise you

In Iran, Hafiz is not only read but used. Many families keep his *Divan* at home. At Nowruz or on Yalda Night they open it at random and read the poem as a sign of what is coming. Many people know his lines by heart and quote them as everyday sayings.

His reach went far west too. Goethe's *West-Eastern Divan* (1819) was inspired by reading him in German translation, and Emerson called him "a poet's poet". His tomb in Shiraz is still a place people visit regularly.

### Where to start reading

Gertrude Bell's *Poems from the Divan of Hafiz* (1897): a small selection in graceful English by the explorer and scholar. Free on Project Gutenberg.

## Jean-Jacques Rousseau

**Jean-Jacques Rousseau** · Geneva, Switzerland · 1712 – 1778 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Rousseau was born in Geneva in 1712, the son of a watchmaker. His mother died nine days after his birth. Apprenticed to an engraver who beat him, he left Geneva as a teenager and drifted through Savoy and France.

In Paris he became a friend of Diderot and wrote for the *Encyclopédie*. Fame came suddenly in 1750, when his essay arguing that the arts and sciences had corrupted naturally good people won a prize. He also wrote music, and his opera *The Village Soothsayer* pleased King Louis XV.

In 1762 he published *The Social Contract* and *Emile*. Both were condemned: his books were burned and warrants issued for his arrest. He spent years moving from refuge to refuge, grew fearful of plots against him, and died at Ermenonville in 1778.

### What he cared about

Rousseau asked why people born good and free end up unequal and unhappy. His themes:

- **Freedom lost in society:** people are born free, yet everywhere live in chains of their own making.
- **Inequality:** private property, he argued, is where social inequality begins.
- **Citizens, not spectators:** a free people takes part in its shared affairs; once citizens stop caring, the state is lost.
- **Feeling, not just years:** the one who has lived most is the one who has most felt life.
- **Vanity:** much unhappiness comes from caring only about our standing against others.

### What might surprise you

The great writer on education gave away his own children. By his own account, he and Thérèse Levasseur, later his wife, had five children, and all were left at a foundling hospital soon after birth, then almost a death sentence. Later in life he said he regretted it.

His picture of the family also rested on women serving men. In *Emile* he argued that girls should be educated to please men, which is why Mary Wollstonecraft attacked him by name. His later books, the *Confessions* and the *Reveries of the Solitary Walker*, turn inward, looking closely at his own feelings and memories.

### Where to start reading

*The Social Contract*, Book I: short chapters, and the famous opening line. Free on Project Gutenberg in English; the app uses its own translations from the French.

## Sei Shōnagon

**Sei Shōnagon** · Japan · about 966 – after 1017 · Japanese Aesthetics

### Who she was

Sei Shōnagon served as a lady-in-waiting to the Empress Teishi around the year 1000, at the Heian court in Kyoto. We do not know her real name. "Sei" comes from the family name of her father, the poet Kiyohara Motosuke, and "Shōnagon" is a court title whose link to her is unclear.

During her years at court she kept the notes that became *The Pillow Book*, finished around 1002. The Empress died in 1000, and after that the records of Sei Shōnagon almost vanish.

One tradition says she remarried and had a daughter. Another says she ended her days poor, as a Buddhist nun. We do not know which is true.

### What she cared about

*The Pillow Book* mixes stories, poems, opinions, and about 164 lists, with headings like "Cheerful Things" or "Dreary Things". Her themes:

- **Small things with big feelings:** a withered flower or an old letter can bring back a whole past.
- **The seasons:** each has its own perfect moment, like spring at dawn or winter snow.
- **Taste:** she judges everything, from good letter paper to bad manners, with total confidence.
- **Honest dislikes:** she lists what annoys her as freely as what delights her.
- **Noticing:** the joy of paying close attention to an ordinary day.

### What might surprise you

She meant the book to be private. According to the story, she left it on a cushion she put out for a guest, who carried it off despite her pleas. It went on to shape a whole Japanese genre, *zuihitsu*, or "assorted writing".

Not everyone liked her. Her rival Murasaki Shikibu, author of *The Tale of Genji*, wrote about her sharply in her diary while admitting her gifts. Many readers still find her arrogant, and many love her sharp wit for exactly the same reason.

### Where to start reading

The passages W. G. Aston translated in *A History of Japanese Literature* (1899): a short taste of her lists, free online. For the whole book, look for a modern complete translation.

## Jean de La Bruyère

**Jean de La Bruyère** · Paris, France · 1645 – 1696 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

La Bruyère was born in Paris in 1645 to a middle-class family. He trained as a lawyer and in 1673 bought a post in the tax office at Caen, which gave him an income and some standing.

Through the churchman Bossuet he found a place in 1684 in the household of the Prince of Condé, one of the greatest nobles in France. He became tutor to the prince's grandson and spent the rest of his life there, watching the powerful up close.

What he saw went into *The Characters* (1688). He was elected to the Académie française in 1693, after three defeats, and died suddenly in 1696.

### What he cared about

*The Characters* is a book of short remarks and sharp portraits of the people of his time. His themes:

- **Forgetting to live:** we do not feel our birth, we suffer our death, and we forget the living in between.
- **The present:** children enjoy the moment, something adults rarely manage.
- **Conversation:** the art lies less in showing your own wit than in helping others find theirs.
- **Knowing when to be quiet:** it is a misfortune to have neither the wit to speak well nor the judgement to stay silent.
- **Vanity and status:** the court's climbers, flatterers, and snobs, drawn without mercy.

### What might surprise you

Readers loved guessing who was who. Copies of "keys" passed around Paris, naming the real people behind his portraits. He denied that any portrait showed a single person, but many recognised themselves and became his enemies, and they blocked his way into the Académie for years.

He mocked people who invented noble ancestors, and he signed his name as one word, Delabruyère, to show it. Yet those who met him describe a silent, watchful, rather awkward man, far from the sharp voice of his book.

### Where to start reading

*The Characters*, the chapters "Of Man" and "Of Society and Conversation": short remarks you can read a few at a time. Free on Project Gutenberg; the app uses its own translations from the French.

## Leo Tolstoy

**Leo Tolstoy** · Yasnaya Polyana, Russia · 1828 – 1910 · Existential Thought

### Who he was

Tolstoy was born in 1828 into a family of Russian counts, on the estate of Yasnaya Polyana south of Moscow. His mother died when he was two and his father when he was nine. He left university without a degree, ran up gambling debts, and joined the army, serving as an artillery officer in the siege of Sevastopol.

In 1862 he married Sophia Behrs, and they had 13 children, eight of whom survived childhood. In the years that followed he wrote *War and Peace* (1869) and *Anna Karenina* (1878), often named among the greatest novels ever written.

Then, in his early fifties, came a crisis of meaning, and a turn to a radical, personal Christianity. He gave up meat and tried to give up his wealth. In 1910, aged 82, he set off on a train journey and died of pneumonia at a small railway station.

### What he cared about

*A Confession* (written 1879–1880) tells how success stopped meaning anything to him, and how he searched for a reason to live. His themes:

- **The question of meaning:** if death is certain, what is the point of what I do today?
- **Success is not an answer:** fame, family, and wealth did not save him from despair.
- **Honesty about despair:** he describes the crisis plainly, without dressing it up.
- **Faith as a way of living:** he came to read the Sermon on the Mount literally, as rules for life.
- **Non-violence:** never resist evil by force, a view that later shaped Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.

### What might surprise you

The author of *War and Peace* came to reject his own great novels, along with most of modern Western culture. His ideas also led the Russian Orthodox Church to excommunicate him in 1901, and the Church's censors cut *A Confession* from the journal that first tried to print it.

His home life suffered as his views hardened. His wish to give away his wealth and the copyrights to his books caused deep conflict with his wife. He was nominated for the Nobel Prize several times and never won it.

### Where to start reading

*A Confession*: short, direct, and personal. Aylmer Maude's English translation is free on Standard Ebooks.

## Niccolò Machiavelli

**Niccolò Machiavelli** · Florence, Italy · 1469 – 1527 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Machiavelli was born in Florence in 1469. From 1498 to 1512, while the Medici family was out of power, he served the Florentine Republic as a senior official in charge of diplomatic and military affairs. His missions let him watch rulers like Cesare Borgia build states up close.

He also raised a citizen militia, which took Pisa in 1509. But in 1512 the Medici returned and he lost his post. Wrongly suspected of plotting against them, he was jailed and tortured, then freed under an amnesty three weeks later.

He retired to his farm outside Florence and became a writer. There, around 1513, he wrote *The Prince*. It was published in 1532, five years after his death.

### What he cared about

Machiavelli wanted to describe politics as it is, not as it should be. His themes:

- **Feared or loved:** if a ruler cannot be both, fear is safer, but he must never be hated.
- **Appearances:** most people judge by what they see; few know who you really are.
- **Act early:** problems are easy to cure but hard to see at first, and easy to see but hard to cure later.
- **Change is hard:** whoever brings a new order makes enemies of all who gained from the old one.
- **Virtù and fortune:** a ruler's skill and daring matter, and so does fortune, which he must always reckon with.

### What might surprise you

The man behind "Machiavellian" was a lifelong republican. His *Discourses on Livy* (around 1517) has been called a founding text of modern republicanism. Some 18th-century readers, Rousseau among them, even read *The Prince* as satire, though most scholars disagree.

Still, the book's hard edges are real. He admired Cesare Borgia and held that fraud, deceit, and violence can be necessary to keep power. In 1559 the Catholic Church put *The Prince* on its list of banned books.

### Where to start reading

*The Prince*, chapters 15 to 18: the famous pages on how rulers should behave. W. K. Marriott's translation (1908) is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Vauvenargues

**Vauvenargues** · Aix-en-Provence, France · 1715 – 1747 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Luc de Clapiers, marquis de Vauvenargues, was born in Aix-en-Provence in 1715, into a noble but poor family. Frail health kept his schooling to a minimum: he never learned Latin or Greek. He joined the army as a young man.

In 1742 he took part in the disastrous siege of Prague. During the winter retreat his legs froze, and he never fully recovered. Hoping for a career as a diplomat, he wrote for two years to ministers and even to King Louis XV, with no success.

Then smallpox left him disfigured, nearly blind, and with a chronic cough. He moved to Paris in 1745 and lived almost alone, seeing a few friends, Voltaire among them. He published his only book in 1746, without his name, and died the next year, aged 31.

### What he cared about

His book gathered an essay on the human mind with hundreds of short reflections and maxims. His themes:

- **The heart thinks too:** great thoughts come from the heart, and reason follows.
- **Living, not fearing death:** the thought of death deceives us when it makes us forget to live.
- **Patience:** he called it the art of hoping.
- **Acting, not only reflecting:** no one makes more mistakes than those who act only after reflection.
- **A good view of people:** where others saw only self-interest, he saw real nobility.

### What might surprise you

He was the friend Voltaire admired, though Voltaire was 20 years older and already famous. Voltaire encouraged him to publish and urged him to bring out an improved second edition.

He is often set beside La Rochefoucauld, but they look at people from opposite sides. La Rochefoucauld finds vanity behind every virtue. Vauvenargues thinks well of human beings and has been called a modern Stoic. His name only appeared on his work in 1797, and his maxims became popular in the 1850s, a century after his death.

### Where to start reading

*Reflections and Maxims*: short lines you can open anywhere. Free in French on Wikisource; the app uses its own translations.

## Heraclitus

**Heraclitus** · Ephesus, Turkey · active about 500 BCE · Classical Greek Philosophy

### Who he was

Heraclitus lived around 500 BCE in Ephesus, a Greek city on the coast of today's Turkey, then part of the Persian Empire. We know very little about him. Most ancient stories about his life were probably invented later from his own sayings.

One story says he gave up the hereditary title of "king" to his younger brother, which at least suggests he came from a leading family. He seems to have had little sympathy for democracy or for the crowd.

He wrote a single book, later known as *On Nature*, and is said to have placed it in the great temple of Artemis. The book is lost. What survives is just over a hundred short fragments quoted by other writers.

### What he cared about

Heraclitus saw a world in constant change, held together by a hidden order he called the *logos*. His themes:

- **Everything flows:** you cannot step into the same river twice, because the water is always new.
- **Opposites belong together:** the way up and the way down are one and the same.
- **Harmony in strife:** conflict and tension are part of how the world holds together.
- **Learning is not understanding:** knowing many facts does not make you wise.
- **Look inward:** "I inquired of myself", and a person's character is their guardian spirit.

### What might surprise you

Even the ancients found him hard. His riddling style earned him the names "the dark" and "the obscure". He was seen as proud and gloomy, and later art paired him as "the weeping philosopher" with Democritus, "the laughing philosopher".

He was also scornful. He mocked Homer, Hesiod, and Pythagoras, along with the religious rites of his day. Yet his few lines shaped Plato, the Stoics, Hegel, Nietzsche, and Heidegger.

### Where to start reading

The fragments themselves: a few pages that take a lifetime. Arthur Fairbanks's translation in *The First Philosophers of Greece* (1898) is free online.

## René Descartes

**René Descartes** · La Haye, France · 1596 – 1650 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Descartes was born in 1596 in Touraine, in France. His mother died when he was a year old. He was a sickly child, studied with the Jesuits at La Flèche, and took a law degree to please his father.

Instead of practising law, he joined the Dutch army as a volunteer to learn military engineering. One cold night in 1619, shut in a room heated by a stove, he had three dreams that he believed revealed a new philosophy to him.

He spent more than 20 years in the Dutch Republic and wrote all his major works there. In 1649 Queen Christina of Sweden invited him to her court. He died in Stockholm the next winter, probably of pneumonia.

### What he cared about

Descartes wanted knowledge built on foundations so firm that nothing could shake them. His themes:

- **Doubt as a method:** accept nothing as true until you see clearly that it is.
- **I think, therefore I am:** even when doubting everything, he could not doubt that he was thinking.
- **Good sense, well used:** everyone has reason; what matters is how we direct it.
- **Master yourself:** change your desires rather than the order of the world.
- **Reading as conversation:** good books let us talk with the best minds of past ages.

### What might surprise you

He was a great mathematician as well as a philosopher. He joined algebra and geometry, and the graph grid with x and y axes, the Cartesian coordinate system, is named after him. When Galileo was condemned in 1633, Descartes put his own book on the physical world in a drawer.

His private life was more ordinary than his reputation. With a servant, Helena Jans van der Strom, he had a daughter, Francine, who died of scarlet fever at five. In Sweden he was asked to teach the queen at 5 a.m. in a cold castle; they met only a few times and did not like each other.

### Where to start reading

*Discourse on Method*: short, personal, and readable in an afternoon. John Veitch's translation (1850) is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Francis Bacon

**Francis Bacon** · London, England · 1561 – 1626 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

Bacon was born in London in 1561, the son of one of Queen Elizabeth I's senior officials. He studied at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he came to find the Aristotle he was taught barren and argumentative. His father died before providing for him, and he spent years in debt.

He trained as a lawyer and rose slowly, then quickly under King James I. He became Attorney General in 1613 and later Lord Chancellor, the highest legal office in England, with the titles Baron Verulam and Viscount St Alban.

In 1621 he was found guilty of corruption. He was fined, held briefly in the Tower of London, and barred from public office. He spent his last years writing and died of pneumonia in 1626.

### What he cared about

Bacon wanted knowledge that came from careful observation, not from old authorities. In his *Essays* he brought the same sharp eye to everyday life. His themes:

- **Learn from nature:** test ideas against what we actually observe, step by step.
- **Beware the mind's traps:** he called our habits of self-deception "idols of the mind".
- **Envy:** it always comes from comparing ourselves with others, especially those close to us.
- **Reading, talking, writing:** each shapes the mind differently, and we need all three.
- **Read with judgement:** some books are to be tasted, some swallowed, and a few chewed and digested.

### What might surprise you

The father of empiricism may have died of an experiment. John Aubrey, who had the story from Thomas Hobbes, says Bacon stopped in the snow to test whether cold could preserve meat as salt does, stuffed a hen with snow, and fell fatally ill. The two events may be only a coincidence.

His public career had dark sides. As Attorney General, he used torture to win a treason conviction. Tried for bribery, he admitted taking gifts from people with cases before him, then a common custom, but insisted they never swayed his judgement.

### Where to start reading

The *Essays* (final edition, 1625): 58 short pieces, a few pages each. "Of Studies" and "Of Envy" are good first choices. Free on Project Gutenberg.
