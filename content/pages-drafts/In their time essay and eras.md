## The opening essay

*Always at the top of the Library. About 400 words. Draft 1: the story is yours, so please correct any detail I got wrong.*

### Before you read: in their time

At school, I had a history teacher who began the school year in an unusual way. In the first lesson, he taught no dates, no kings, and no battles. For two hours, he talked instead about how people of the past saw the world: what they feared, what they took for granted, what they could not even imagine. Historians call this the history of mentalities. His point was simple. You cannot understand what people did until you understand the world they did it in.

The same is true of ideas. A sentence written two thousand years ago can feel as if it was written for you this morning. Some ideas really are like that: Seneca's warning that we waste our lives, not that life is short, needs no footnote. Others only show their full weight once you know what surrounded them.

When Epicurus opened his school in Athens, he welcomed women as students, in a city where women were kept out of public life. Today that sounds ordinary; then, it was a quiet revolution. When Socrates kept asking Athenians what they really meant by justice or courage, he was not playing a game. In 399 BCE, a jury sentenced him to death.

It works the other way too. Aristotle, one of the sharpest minds who ever lived, argued that some people are slaves by nature. To him, that was common sense. To us, it is plainly wrong. Knowing his world does not excuse it, but it helps us see how a brilliant person can share the blind spots of his time, and it invites a harder question: which of our own certainties will look like that one day?

Some ideas changed our world precisely because they shocked theirs. Mary Wollstonecraft's claim that women seem weaker only because they are denied an education was radical in 1792. The fact that it sounds obvious now is, in part, her doing.

So as you read the thinkers in this Library, try asking two questions. What did this mean to the people who first heard it? And what does it mean to me, here, today? "In their time" is here to help with the first. The second is yours.

## The eras

Thirteen era pages cover all 59 thinkers and the 4 texts. Each thinker belongs to exactly one era, chosen by where and when they did their main work. Eras are grouped by world, not by school, so one era can mix paths, and one path can span several eras.

Each era page would answer the same few questions: How did people live? What did they believe about the gods, death, family, and work? Who counted, and who was left out? What was radical in this era's thinkers, and what still echoes today?

## Era page: Ancient Greece

**Ancient Greece** · 6th–3rd centuries BCE · Heraclitus, Plato, Aristotle, Epicurus

### In one line

Small, proud cities where a few free men argued about everything, and most people had no say at all.

### How people lived

Greece was not one country but hundreds of city-states, each with its own laws, gods, and army. Athens, the best known, became a democracy around 508 BCE: citizens voted directly in an assembly. It was also a city at war for much of this time. In 404 BCE it lost a long war against Sparta, and a harsh regime of thirty oligarchs briefly took power.

After Alexander the Great died in 323 BCE, the cities lost their independence to large kingdoms. Athens was defeated in 322 BCE, and Macedonian soldiers held its port. This is the world Epicurus taught in, from around 306 BCE: a city that could no longer steer its own fate.

### What they believed

Greek religion had no holy book, no single priesthood, and no creed. It was something you did more than something you believed: sacrifices at altars, libations of wine, and festivals, which filled some 140 days of the Athenian year. People consulted oracles, above all the priestess of Apollo at Delphi, and most imagined the dead as spirits in an underworld.

Religion was bound up with the city itself. That is why, in 399 BCE, Socrates was charged with failing to honour the city's gods and with corrupting the young. A jury of about 500 citizens found him guilty, 280 to 221, and sentenced him to death.

### Who counted, and who was left out

Only adult male citizens could vote. Athenian women had no legal standing of their own: a father, then a husband, acted for them, and girls often married around fourteen, to men twice their age. Religious festivals were almost the only public life open to them. Foreign residents, called metics, paid a special tax and could neither vote nor own land.

And Athens ran on slavery. There may have been some 80,000 slaves in the city in the 6th and 5th centuries BCE, and most free families owned at least one. The worst work was in the silver mines. Few Greeks questioned this. Aristotle called slaves "living tools"; one of the rare dissenters, the orator Alcidamas, answered that "nature has made nobody a slave".

### Ahead of their time

Most Athenians took the gods, customs, and values of their city as given. Socrates kept asking what those ideas really meant, and in 399 BCE the city put him to death for it.

In most Greek cities, women were kept out of public life and learning. Epicurus welcomed women into his school. Most Greeks honoured the gods through public rites. Heraclitus mocked some of those rites openly.

### What still echoes today

The word "democracy", the habit of asking for reasons, and the question of how to live a good life all come from these cities. So does a warning: Aristotle's claim that some people are slaves by nature was used, centuries later, to defend the enslavement of Native Americans.

## Era page: Japan, from the Heian court to the age of the samurai

**Japan, from the Heian court to the age of the samurai** · 10th–14th centuries · Sei Shōnagon, Kamo no Chōmei, Kenkō

### In one line

A refined court that prized beauty, a country slowly taken over by warriors, and a Buddhism that believed the world was in decline.

### How people lived

In 794 the emperor moved his capital to Heian-kyō, today's Kyoto, and gave the age its name. Over time, real power passed to a great noble family, the Fujiwara. The court was tiny: perhaps 5,000 aristocrats in a land of some five million people. In that small world, beauty was seen as part of what made a person good.

Outside the capital, a new class of provincial warriors, the samurai, grew stronger. A civil war between two warrior clans ended in 1185, and the victor, Minamoto no Yoritomo, set up a military government at Kamakura. The court survived, but the warriors ruled. They held off two Mongol invasions, in 1274 and 1281, before their own regime fell in 1333.

### What they believed

Many Japanese Buddhists believed they lived in *mappō*, the age of decline of the Buddha's teaching, which by their count began around 1052. This belief helped give rise to new schools of Buddhism in the Kamakura years, such as the Pure Land school of Hōnen and the school of Nichiren. Zen also took root, brought by Eisai and Dōgen.

Disaster was never far away. In *Hōjōki*, Chōmei describes a great fire in 1177, a whirlwind and a forced move of the capital in 1180, and a famine in which one monk counted 42,300 bodies in the capital alone.

### Who counted, and who was left out

The literature of this age was written by and for a tiny elite, a few thousand people in a land of millions. Within it, women were mostly kept from Chinese learning, the prestige writing of men. But they wrote in Japanese, in a script called "women's hand", and much of the great writing in Japanese came from court women.

### Ahead of their time

Women at court were kept from Chinese learning, the writing that carried the most prestige. Sei Shōnagon wrote in Japanese instead, and filled her book with her own opinions, likes, and dislikes, with unusual freedom.

People who turned away from the world usually went to live in a temple. Chōmei took Buddhist vows in 1204, but chose another path: he built a tiny hut in the hills outside the capital and lived there alone. In *Hōjōki* he set down his life in the hut, after the disasters he had seen.

### What still echoes today

Kenkō's question still speaks to us: should we look at the moon only when it is full, or at flowers only in full bloom? These writers taught that things can be beautiful because they pass.

## Open points for Théo

- **Your voice in the essay.** It is written in the first person ("At school, I had…"). Should it stay as your story, signed or unsigned, or become "we" like the rest of the app? I'd keep it personal; it's the warmest part.
- **Simone Weil alone in era 13.** One option is to merge eras 10 and 13 into "Europe and Russia, 19th–20th centuries". I'd keep her era separate, because the world wars changed how people saw almost everything. The era page can say plainly why she is alone: most 20th-century thinkers, such as Sartre, de Beauvoir, and Camus, are still under copyright, so the app cannot quote them yet (the Existential Thought path page already says this). As their works enter the public domain, they can join her.
- **Edge cases.** Boethius lived after the last Western emperor fell, so era 2 runs "to its fall". Tolstoy, William James, and Tagore lived into the 20th century, but did their main work before it. Heraclitus lived in a Greek city under Persian rule. Each is placed by where and when they did their main work.
- **Era 4 dates.** The four Indian texts span roughly a thousand years, and their dates are uncertain. The page would say so plainly rather than give false precision.
- **The Library view.** The era pages could sit on a horizontal timeline, reusing the About page timeline. This is the timeline idea from the path pages, finding its natural home.
- **Sourcing.** Era pages are social history, so every claim gets checked, and claims historians still dispute are named as such. Uncomfortable facts, like slavery and the exclusion of women, are named plainly, in every era where they apply.
