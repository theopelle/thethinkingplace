## Topic page: Habit and practice

**Habit and practice** · theme: Habit and practice · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Are we what we repeatedly do?

### How the traditions see it

**Epictetus and Aristotle (Stoicism, Classical Greek Philosophy).** If you want to be a good reader, said Epictetus, read; if a writer, write. Nothing great happens suddenly, not even a grape or a fig. Aristotle said the same of goodness: we become just by doing just acts.

**Confucius (Confucianism).** Confucius compared learning to building a mound, one basket of earth at a time. If you stop one basket short, the stopping is your own doing; if you add one basket, you have moved forward. People start out much alike; practice sets them apart.

**The Dhammapada (Buddhist Philosophy).** The *Dhammapada* compares training the mind to a fletcher straightening an arrow. Small acts add up, like a water jar filling drop by drop, both for good and for bad. If you do something good, do it again, and take delight in it.

**Laozi and Zhuangzi (Taoism).** Every great thing starts small: the journey of a thousand li begins with a single step. Zhuangzi's cook has used the same knife for nineteen years. After long practice he no longer sees the whole ox, and works with his mind, following the natural gaps.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them see small, repeated acts as the way a person changes. They differ on the goal. Confucius and Epictetus speak of steady effort; Zhuangzi's cook shows effort turning into ease. Kenkō and Hume add a warning: habits and customs also hold us, and few things are harder to give up.

## Topic page: Suffering and resilience

**Suffering and resilience** · theme: Suffering and resilience · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What do we do with pain we cannot avoid?

### How the traditions see it

**Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, and Boethius (Stoicism).** Marcus Aurelius asked to be like a headland that the waves keep breaking on, while it stands firm. Epictetus advised wishing for things to happen as they do, not as we would like. In prison, Boethius wrote that nothing is wretched unless we think it so.

**Rumi and Hafiz (Persian and Sufi Poetry).** The Sufi poets see pain as part of the way. Rumi calls it a treasure that holds mercies, and the *Masnavi* opens with a reed flute crying for the reed bed it was cut from. Hafiz says no one reaches joy without passing through hardship.

**Nietzsche and Dostoevsky (Existential Thought).** Nietzsche's answer was *amor fati*, love of fate: wanting nothing to be different, the hard parts included. Dostoevsky went further, calling suffering the origin of consciousness itself.

**Weil and Mill (Existential Thought, Enlightenment Ethics).** Others look outward. Simone Weil said those who suffer need, above all, people able to pay them attention. And Mill believed most of the great causes of suffering can be reduced by human care and effort.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Many of them say suffering can teach and deepen us, as Vivekananda put it: misery is sometimes a greater teacher than happiness. They differ on where the answer lies. The Stoics look to our own judgement, the Sufi poets to love and longing, Weil to other people, and Mill to changing the world itself.

## Topic page: Work and action

**Work and action** · theme: Work and action · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

How should we act, and what should we expect from it?

### How the traditions see it

**The Bhagavad Gita and Vivekananda (Indian Philosophy).** In the *Gita*, Krishna tells Arjuna that his business is with the action alone, never with its fruits. It is better to do your own task imperfectly than someone else's well. Vivekananda put it simply: work without stopping, but without attachment.

**Marcus Aurelius (Stoicism).** Even an emperor found it hard to get up in the morning. His reminder to himself: I am rising to do the work of a human being. He also told himself to stop talking about what a good man should be, and simply be one.

**Laozi and Zhuangzi (Taoism).** The Taoists value action that does not force. Governing a great state, says the *Tao Te Ching*, is like cooking small fish: stir too much and they fall apart. Zhuangzi's cook works with the grain of things, and his knife stays sharp for nineteen years.

**Confucius and Voltaire (Confucianism, Enlightenment Ethics).** Confucius praised the person who acts first and speaks afterwards. At the end of Voltaire's *Candide*, after war, shipwreck, and earthquake, the hero stops arguing about whether all is for the best. "We must cultivate our garden."

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them trust deeds over words. They differ on effort and reward. The *Gita* and the Stoics speak of duty done well whatever the result; the Taoists warn against forcing things; Voltaire suggests that useful work close to home may be the best answer to questions we cannot solve.

## Topic page: Purpose and meaning

**Purpose and meaning** · theme: Purpose and meaning · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What is a life for?

### How the traditions see it

**Aristotle (Classical Greek Philosophy).** Aristotle noticed that everything we do aims at some good, and those goods point to a final one: happiness, the thing we want for its own sake. For him, the question of meaning had an answer: a life of reason and virtue, lived well.

**Tolstoy and William James (Existential Thought).** Tolstoy had fame, family, and wealth when his life "came to a standstill": he could no longer see what any of it was for. William James answered the same fear in a lecture called "Is Life Worth Living?". We live fully, he said, only by risking ourselves on what we believe.

**Mill (Enlightenment Ethics).** At twenty, Mill asked himself whether reaching all his goals would make him happy, and an honest voice said no. He came out of the crisis with a lesson: happiness comes to those who aim at something other than their own happiness.

**George Eliot, Dickinson, and Tagore (Humanists and Essayists, Indian Philosophy).** Many find meaning in what we do for others. "What do we live for," asks a character in *Middlemarch*, "if it is not to make life less difficult to each other?" Dickinson would not live in vain if she could stop one heart from breaking, and Tagore says we earn our life by giving it.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Most of them say meaning comes from something larger than the self, whether virtue, a cause, or other people. They differ on whether it is given or made. Aristotle finds it in human nature; Nietzsche says you must become what you are; Zhuangzi gently mocks the whole idea, praising a useless tree that lives long because nobody wants it.

## Topic page: Nature and the universe

**Nature and the universe** · theme: Nature and the universe · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What is the world made of, and where do we fit in it?

### How the traditions see it

**Laozi and Zhuangzi (Taoism).** The Tao, the way of nature, cannot be fully named or explained. Its best image is water, which helps everything, takes the lowest place, and still wears away the hardest stone. A person lives well by following that way, not by forcing it.

**Lucretius and Epicurus (Epicureanism).** For the Epicureans, everything is made of atoms moving in empty space; worlds form by chance, without any need for gods. Lucretius wrote that fear comes from not knowing the causes of things. Understanding nature, he said, is the cure for that fear.

**Marcus Aurelius and Epictetus (Stoicism).** The Stoics saw the opposite: one living whole, shaped by reason, in which every part is connected. Marcus Aurelius told himself to see the universe as a single being with one substance and one soul. Epictetus reminded his students how small a part of it each of us is.

**Kant and Pascal (Enlightenment Ethics, Existential Thought).** Two things filled Kant with ever-growing awe: the starry heavens above and the moral law within. Pascal saw human beings as reeds, the weakest thing in nature, but reeds that think, and our greatness lies in that thought.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them say that understanding nature changes how we live in it. They split on whether it has a purpose. The Stoics see a rational order; the Epicureans see atoms and chance; the Taoists see a way that cannot be put into words. The Japanese writers add something simpler: notice it, from the dawn to the flowers.

## Topic page: Death and mortality

**Death and mortality** · theme: Death and mortality · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Should we think about death, and can we stop fearing it?

### How the traditions see it

**Epicurus and Lucretius (Epicureanism).** Epicurus argued that death is nothing to us: where death is, we are not, and where we are, death is not. Lucretius asked why we should not leave life like a guest leaving a banquet, full and content. On the day he died, in great pain, Epicurus wrote to a friend that the memory of their conversations outweighed it all.

**Marcus Aurelius and Montaigne (Stoicism, Humanists and Essayists).** Marcus Aurelius reminded himself not to act as if he had ten thousand years. Montaigne took an old saying for an essay title: to study philosophy is to learn to die. Whoever has learned to die, he said, has unlearned how to be a slave.

**Zhuangzi (Taoism).** When Zhuangzi's wife died, a friend found him beating on a bowl and singing. He had grieved like anyone, he explained, then saw her death as one more change, like spring turning to summer, autumn, and winter. Tagore says something close: death belongs to life as birth does.

**Omar Khayyam and Kenkō (Persian and Sufi Poetry, Japanese Aesthetics).** For Khayyam, death is a door with no key and a veil no one sees past, so we should make the most of the days we have. Kenkō notes that death does not wait its turn like the seasons; it presses on us from behind.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

Most of them say that facing death honestly makes life clearer and fear smaller. They split on how much to think about it. Montaigne and the Stoics advise keeping it in mind; Spinoza answered that a free person thinks of death least of all, and that wisdom is a meditation on life.

## Topic page: Love

**Love** · theme: Love · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

What is love, and what does it ask of us?

### How the traditions see it

**Plato (Classical Greek Philosophy).** In Plato's *Symposium*, guests at a dinner take turns praising love. One tells a myth: humans were once whole beings, cut in two, and love is the desire and pursuit of that lost whole. Then Socrates, quoting his teacher Diotima, describes love as a journey from one beautiful body towards ever purer beauty.

**Rumi, Hafiz, and Ibn Arabi (Persian and Sufi Poetry).** For the Sufi poets, human love and love of God mirror each other. Love hurts, and the pain is the proof of it. Hafiz admits he thought love would be easy, then found it a hard road. Ibn Arabi wrote that a heart grown in love can take on every form.

**Tagore and the Dhammapada (Indian Philosophy, Buddhist Philosophy).** Tagore saw love as the way we truly live in the world: when a person loves, giving becomes a joy, like a tree letting go of ripe fruit. The *Dhammapada* gives love a harder task: hatred never ends through hatred, only through love.

**Dostoevsky (Existential Thought).** Dostoevsky saw how easy it is to love in theory. Love in action, says a monk in *The Brothers Karamazov*, is harsh and dreadful compared with love in dreams. The same monk tells of a doctor who found that the more he loved humanity in general, the less he loved people in particular.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them see love as more than a feeling: it changes the one who loves. They differ on its object. Plato and the Sufi poets point beyond the person towards something higher; Dostoevsky and Montaigne bring it back to one real person, loved, as Montaigne said of his friend, "because it was he, because it was I".

## Topic page: Beauty and art

**Beauty and art** · theme: Beauty and art · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Is beauty out there, or in the eye of the beholder?

### How the traditions see it

**Kenkō and Okakura (Japanese Aesthetics).** The Japanese writers love what is unfinished. Kenkō thought it better to leave a little imperfection, since perfect uniformity is dull. In *The Book of Tea*, Okakura says true beauty is found by the one who completes the incomplete in their own mind: what is left unsaid gives the viewer a part to play.

**Hume (Enlightenment Ethics).** Hume started from the view that beauty is no quality in things, but exists in the mind that looks at them. Yet he did not leave it there. Some judgements are better than others, he argued, and the standard of taste is the shared verdict of rare, experienced, unprejudiced critics. Studying beauty, he added, softens the temper.

**Laozi (Taoism).** The *Tao Te Ching* sees a hidden cost in naming beauty: as soon as everyone knows what is beautiful, they also know what is ugly. Each idea brings its opposite with it.

**Nietzsche and Emerson (Existential Thought, Humanists and Essayists).** For Nietzsche, the greatest art is to "give style" to your own character, shaping strengths and weaknesses into one whole. Emerson saw in every work of genius our own rejected thoughts, coming back to us with a kind of borrowed majesty.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them link beauty to the person who sees it, not only to the object. They differ on how far. Hume searches for a standard that lets us say some tastes are better; the Japanese writers make the viewer a partner in the work; Nietzsche turns the art inward, onto how we shape our lives.

## Topic page: Solitude and stillness

**Solitude and stillness** · theme: Solitude and stillness · \[n\] thoughts so far

### The question in one line

Why is it so hard to sit still, and what do we find when we do?

### How the traditions see it

**Pascal (Existential Thought).** Pascal thought all human misfortune comes from one thing: we cannot stay quietly in a room. We chase distraction to avoid thinking about ourselves, and that distraction is itself our greatest misery.

**Marcus Aurelius (Stoicism).** While ruling an empire, Marcus longed for a house in the country or by the sea, as rich Romans had. Then he reminded himself that he could retreat into his own mind whenever he chose, and that there is no quieter place.

**Laozi and Zhuangzi (Taoism).** Muddy water, says the *Tao Te Ching*, becomes clear if you let it be still. Zhuangzi says the wise use the mind like a mirror: it grasps nothing and refuses nothing, and reflects what comes without holding on.

**Kenkō and Chōmei (Japanese Aesthetics).** The Japanese recluses praised a quiet life away from the crowd. Kenkō asked how anyone could find leisure tiresome: free from cares, it is good simply to be alone. From his small hut, Chōmei watched the moon at his window and thought of people of old.

### Where they agree, and where they don't

All of them see stillness as the place where the mind clears. They differ on how alone to be. Schopenhauer and Nietzsche praise solitude itself, and the *Dhammapada* says it is better to travel alone than with a fool. For Marcus, though, the retreat is inward and can be taken anywhere, even in the middle of public life.
