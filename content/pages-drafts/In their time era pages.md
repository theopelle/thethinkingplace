## Era page: Rome, from the Republic to its fall

**Rome, from the Republic to its fall** · 1st century BCE–6th century CE · Lucretius, Seneca, Epictetus, Marcus Aurelius, Boethius

### In one line

A huge empire that brought peace to the Mediterranean for two centuries, run on slave labour, where philosophy became a practical guide for living well under powers you could not control.

### How people lived

In 27 BCE the Senate gave Octavian the title Augustus, and he became the first emperor. The roughly 200 years that followed are known as the Roman Peace. At its height, the empire held perhaps 55 to 60 million people, between a sixth and a quarter of everyone alive.

People spoke Latin in the West and Greek in the East. Rome, Alexandria, and Antioch were the largest cities, each about twice the size of any European city in the early 1600s. Trade routes grew fast in the first two centuries CE.

### What they believed

Romans worshipped many gods, and later also their emperors. Then Christianity spread. In 313 Constantine gave Christians the right to worship openly, in 380 Theodosius made Christianity the state religion, and in 391 he closed the old temples.

Among the educated, Stoicism was a guide to daily life. It taught that you control only your own judgements and choices, and that the rest is not yours to command. That message spoke both to a man born a slave and to an emperor.

### Who counted, and who was left out

Rome was a slave society. Slaves came from war, piracy, kidnapping, and birth. Caesar alone sold 53,000 people from one tribe. By law a slave was property, with no right to sue or make contracts. Many were freed, and some freedmen grew rich, but their own path to high office stayed closed.

Women were citizens but could not vote or hold office. They could own property, make wills, and divorce, and many upper-class women were well educated. In 212 CE, citizenship was extended to all free people in the empire.

### Ahead of their time

Romans treated a slave as property, not a person. Epictetus, born a slave around 50 CE, taught the opposite: no one can own your judgements and choices, so freedom does not depend on your legal status.

Roman society ranked people sharply by birth and status. Yet the emperor Marcus Aurelius drew on the teaching of this former slave in his own thinking.

### What still echoes today

In 476 the last Western emperor was forced to step down. Some fifty years later, Boethius, a senior official under the Gothic king Theodoric, was arrested for treason and executed in 524. In prison he wrote *The Consolation of Philosophy*.

The question these thinkers kept asking is still ours: how do you stay steady when events, bosses, and luck are not in your hands?

## Era page: China of the Hundred Schools

**China of the Hundred Schools** · 6th–3rd century BCE · Confucius, Laozi, Mencius, Zhuangzi

### In one line

Centuries of war between rival states, when rulers hired wandering thinkers to tell them how to bring back order, and China produced more schools of thought than at almost any other time.

### How people lived

The Zhou kings had once ruled over many lords. After their court moved east in 771 BCE, their power faded, and a few strong states swallowed the weaker ones. From about 475 to 221 BCE, the Warring States period, war never really stopped.

War grew in size. Iron weapons replaced bronze, and armies of chariots gave way to masses of foot soldiers with swords and crossbows. Battles could last months. Some states built the first real bureaucracies, run by officials chosen for their work rather than their birth.

### What they believed

Since the Zhou, people believed Heaven gave the right to rule to a virtuous king, and could take it back from a bad one. The big question of the age was practical: how do you end the chaos? Every school had its answer.

Confucius (551–479 BCE) said order begins with good people in right relationships. Mozi argued for equal care for all and against wars of attack. The Daoist texts linked to Laozi advised living with the grain of nature and ruling by doing less. The Legalists said people are selfish, so only strict laws and punishments work.

### Who counted, and who was left out

Society was strongly patriarchal. The ideal was "men plough, women weave", and a woman's place was inside the home. Girls from about age ten were taught to obey father, then husband, then sons. Daughters were often seen as worth less than sons.

The records still show women advising rulers, judging guests, and writing poetry. One Zhou king even named his wife among his nine ministers. Most of what we know concerns noble families. The lives of ordinary farmers left few traces.

### Ahead of their time

Under the Zhou, land and power passed down through noble families. In this era, a teacher with no title could travel from court to court and win a post as an adviser on government, war, or diplomacy. Some states began to choose officials for their work, not their family.

Kings claimed to rule with Heaven's favour. Mencius, one of the boldest thinkers of his age, turned this around. He ranked the people first and the ruler last, and said a ruler who harms the state should be replaced.

### What still echoes today

In 221 BCE the state of Qin conquered the others, and its king declared himself the first emperor. In 213 BCE the Qin ordered many books burned, including writings of the Hundred Schools. Historians still argue about how much was really lost.

The Han dynasty that followed blended Confucian and Legalist ideas into a system of government that lasted, in its main lines, until the late 19th century. Questions about merit, duty, and good leadership from these schools are still part of how millions of people think about work and family.

## Era page: Ancient India

**Ancient India** · about 7th century BCE–2nd century CE · The Upanishads, the Dhammapada, the Bhagavad Gita, the Questions of King Milinda

### In one line

An age of forest teachers, wandering monks, and royal debates, when India asked who we really are beneath our roles, and how to act well in a world of endless rebirth.

### A word on dates

Dates in this era are uncertain, often by centuries. These texts were spoken and memorised long before they were written down, and many grew over time. Scholars place the oldest Upanishads around the 7th to 6th century BCE, and the Bhagavad Gita anywhere from the 2nd century BCE to the 2nd century CE. We give ranges, not exact years.

### How people lived

In the Ganges plain, kingdoms grew, and kings held court with learned priests. One famous Upanishad describes a contest of ten sages at the court of King Janaka, debating the nature of reality before the king.

Alongside the priests lived the *shramanas*, people who left home and family to seek truth as wandering ascetics. Several such movements already existed by the 6th century BCE. The Buddha and the Jain teacher Mahavira came from this world in the 5th century BCE.

### What they believed

The Vedas, old hymns and rituals kept by priests, were the core of religious life. The Upanishads turned inward. They taught that *Atman*, the inner self, and *Brahman*, the reality behind everything, are one and the same.

Ideas of karma and rebirth took shape in this period, perhaps first among the ascetics. Many shramana groups rejected the authority of the Vedas and their rituals.

### Who counted, and who was left out

An old hymn describes society in four classes: priests, rulers and warriors, farmers and merchants, and labourers and servants. Scholars see this, in early times, more as an ideal than a fixed reality. People outside the four classes were treated as outcastes.

Women appear in the debates, if rarely. Gargi questions the sage Yajnavalkya at Janaka's court, and his wife Maitreyi asks him what makes a person immortal. They are the exception in texts written and kept by men.

### Ahead of their time

In a society organised by class, many held that birth is what makes a person a priest. Early Buddhist texts disagreed. In one dialogue, a priest is led to agree that what truly counts is virtue and wisdom, not birth.

Many seekers of the time believed the spiritual life meant leaving home and family behind. The Bhagavad Gita offered another way. Krishna tells the warrior Arjuna to do his duty in the world, but without craving the results. Action itself can be a spiritual path.

### What still echoes today

In the 2nd century BCE, Indo-Greek kings ruled in the north-west. The *Questions of King Milinda* records, or imagines, a long dialogue between one of them, Menander, and the Buddhist monk Nagasena. It is an early meeting of two worlds, built on patient questions.

The Gita's advice to act fully while letting go of the outcome still speaks to anyone who works hard for results they cannot control.

## Era page: The medieval Islamic world

**The medieval Islamic world** · 11th–14th century · Omar Khayyam, al-Ghazali, Ibn Arabi, Rumi, Sa'di, Hafiz

### In one line

A world of great cities, schools, and libraries, broken by the Mongol invasions, where scholars weighed reason against faith and poets turned the love of God into some of the most loved verse ever written.

### How people lived

By the 11th century, Baghdad had been a centre of learning for centuries. Scholars there had translated Plato, Aristotle, Galen, and Ptolemy from Greek into Arabic. Paper, which came from China in the 8th century, made books cheaper and easier to spread.

Power had passed to Turkish rulers, the Seljuks. Their vizier Nizam al-Mulk founded a network of colleges, the first in Baghdad in 1065. Omar Khayyam, born in Nishapur in 1048, worked for the Seljuk sultan, building an observatory and reforming the Persian calendar. He was also a serious mathematician, who developed the first general theory of cubic equations.

### What they believed

Islam shaped law, learning, and daily life. Within it, two paths met and sometimes clashed: the jurists, who studied the law, and the Sufis, who sought closeness to God through love, prayer, and the remembrance of God.

From the 12th century, Sufi brotherhoods formed and spread across the Muslim world. Persian poets such as Rumi, Sa'di, and Hafiz gave this inner search its most famous voice.

### Who counted, and who was left out

The colleges were open only to men. Women from leading city families were often taught at home, and many earned certificates in religious studies, calligraphy, or poetry. Some women worked as physicians in hospitals.

Slavery was part of life, including in the army. *Mamluks* were boys bought, often from Turkic lands, converted, and trained as soldiers. Freed as adults, they formed a ruling class, and from 1250 they ruled Egypt and Syria themselves.

### Ahead of their time

The jurists, who studied the law, and the Sufis, who sought God through love, were often at odds. Al-Ghazali, a professor at the great college in Baghdad, refused to choose. He brought law and Sufi practice together, and helped Sufism spread.

To many readers, the language of wine and drunkenness had no place in religion. Sufi poets such as Hafiz used it anyway, as an image of the love of God. Some critics doubted whether this was still Islam.

### What still echoes today

In 1219 the Mongols invaded. They destroyed great cities such as Merv, Nishapur, and Herat. In 1258 they took Baghdad, killed the caliph, and massacred its people, an event often seen as the end of the Golden Age of Islamic learning.

Yet much of the poetry was written in or after these years of loss. Rumi's and Hafiz's verses are still read, quoted, and sung today, far beyond the Muslim world.

## Era page: Renaissance Europe

**Renaissance Europe** · 14th–16th century · Petrarch, Erasmus, Machiavelli, Montaigne, Francis Bacon

### In one line

After the plague, Europe went back to the books of Greece and Rome to find a better way to live, and found itself questioning the Church, the prince, and its own customs.

### How people lived

The Black Death reached Europe in 1347. In a few years it killed perhaps 30 to 60 percent of the population, by different estimates. Workers became scarce and wages rose. The old order of lords and peasants began to loosen.

Italy was a patchwork of rich, rival city-states. Florence, where the Renaissance began, was run by merchant families such as the Medici, who paid artists like Leonardo and Michelangelo. From about 1440, printing spread from one shop in Mainz to some 270 cities. By 1500, more than 20 million books had been printed.

### What they believed

Nearly everyone was Christian, and the Church shaped every part of life. The humanists did not reject faith. They wanted to go back to the sources: ancient texts read in their original languages, for lessons in how to live and speak well.

Petrarch (1304–1374) hunted through old libraries for lost Latin works by Cicero and Seneca. Erasmus, the most printed author of the early 1500s, published the New Testament in Greek in 1516. He wanted the Church reformed from within and stayed Catholic all his life.

### Who counted, and who was left out

When the plague came, Jews were falsely accused of poisoning wells. In 1349 about 2,000 were murdered in Strasbourg alone, and by 1351 hundreds of Jewish communities had been destroyed. Many fled east to Poland.

Women rarely appear in the record of the age. A few broke through, like the painter Sofonisba Anguissola. After 1492, Europeans also met the peoples of the Americas for the first time.

### Ahead of their time

Europeans took it for granted that their own world was the civilised one. Montaigne wrote about the Tupinambá of Brazil, who ate their dead enemies, and compared their customs with the cruelty of Europe in his own day. He judged both by the same standard.

Writers on government usually told princes how a good ruler should behave. Machiavelli lost his post when the Medici returned to Florence in 1512, and was imprisoned and tortured. In 1513 he drafted *The Prince*, which looked instead at how rulers really gain and keep power.

### What still echoes today

In 1517 Luther challenged the Pope, and Europe split. In France, wars between Catholics and Protestants ran from 1562 to 1598 and cost perhaps 2 to 4 million lives. In 1572, thousands of Protestants were killed in the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre.

Montaigne lived through all of it. He withdrew to his tower in 1571 to write his *Essays*, then served as mayor of Bordeaux, respected by both sides. His habit of doubting his own certainty is the era's most useful gift.

## Era page: Europe in the 17th century

**Europe in the 17th century** · about 1600–1700 · Descartes, Hobbes, Pascal, Spinoza, Locke, La Rochefoucauld, La Bruyère

### In one line

A century of religious war, revolution, and new science, when thinkers looked for a certainty that no church or king could guarantee anymore.

### How people lived

From 1618 to 1648, the Thirty Years' War tore through central Europe. It started as a fight between Catholics and Protestants and became a contest of crowns. Historians disagree on the toll, but millions died, most of them from plague, hunger, and disease. Some German regions lost more than half their people.

England had its own civil wars. In 1649 Parliament executed King Charles I, and the country became a republic for a time. The king returned in 1660, and in 1688 another king was pushed out in favour of a monarchy limited by Parliament.

### What they believed

Christianity was still the frame of life, but there were now several churches, each sure it was right. Living next to people of another faith was a new and dangerous problem. The Dutch Republic tolerated many faiths, as long as they were practised quietly.

At the same time, a new science was reading nature in the language of mathematics. In 1633 the Church found Galileo strongly suspect of heresy for teaching that the Earth moves around the Sun. He had to reject his views and spent the rest of his life under house arrest.

### Who counted, and who was left out

Few women had a public voice in philosophy, but some entered the debate by letter. From 1643, Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia wrote to Descartes until his death. Her first question went to the heart of his system: how can a mind that has no body move a body?

The new ideas of freedom had blind spots. Locke argued that people have natural rights to life, liberty, and property. Yet he also invested for a time in a company that traded enslaved Africans, and helped draft a colonial constitution that gave free men total power over their slaves. Historians still debate how large his role was.

### Ahead of their time

Most of Europe was ruled by kings with near-total power. In *Leviathan* (1651), Hobbes still defended a strong ruler, but on new grounds: people agree to obey one power to escape the horror of civil war. Locke went further, arguing that government rests on the consent of the governed.

Communities expected their members to share their faith. Spinoza did not, and in 1656, at 23, the Jewish community of Amsterdam expelled him for heresy. He made his living grinding lenses, published his political treatise in 1670 under a false name and place, and left most of his work to be printed after his death.

### What still echoes today

Two questions from this century are still open. How do you know what you know, when authorities disagree? And what do rulers owe the people they rule?

The French moralists of the time, La Rochefoucauld and La Bruyère, asked a third, more personal one: how much of what we call virtue is really self-interest in disguise?

## Era page: The Enlightenment

**The Enlightenment** · 18th century · Voltaire, Émilie du Châtelet, Hume, Adam Smith, Rousseau, Diderot, Vauvenargues, Kant, Chamfort, Mary Wollstonecraft

### In one line

The century that told people to think for themselves and put reason, rights, and freedom at the centre of public life, while leaving out much of humanity.

### How people lived

Most Europeans still lived under kings. Some rulers, such as Frederick the Great of Prussia and Catherine the Great of Russia, took up new ideas and called themselves enlightened. Ideas travelled through salons, coffeehouses, scientific academies, and a fast-growing world of print.

In Paris, elite women such as Madame Geoffrin hosted salons where writers met to argue. From 1751 to 1772, Diderot and d'Alembert, with about 150 others, published the *Encyclopédie*, 35 volumes meant to gather all human knowledge.

### What they believed

Christianity was still the official faith almost everywhere, but many writers questioned the power of churches. Their faith was in reason and in evidence. Kant summed up the spirit of the age in two Latin words, *sapere aude*: dare to know.

Thinking freely had a price. In France, writers fought the censors and were sometimes jailed or forced into exile. Voltaire spent time in the Bastille twice, and one of his books was publicly burned. Many banned books were printed in the Dutch Republic and smuggled into France.

### Who counted, and who was left out

The age of rights was also an age of the Atlantic slave trade. Over the centuries of that trade, between 10 and 12.5 million Africans were forced onto ships to the Americas, and up to two million died on the crossing. Many countries that embraced the new ideas excluded slavery from them.

Women were rarely treated as equals in these debates. Émilie du Châtelet was once thrown out of a Paris café where scholars met, and came back dressed as a man.

### Ahead of their time

Women could attend the Academy of Sciences in Paris only as listeners. Yet in 1739, Émilie du Châtelet's essay on fire became the first work by a woman the Academy published. Her French translation of Newton, finished in 1749, the year she died, is still the standard one.

Many writers, Rousseau among them, held that women should obey men. Mary Wollstonecraft disagreed. In 1792, in *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, she argued that women, given a real education, would be thinking people and better citizens, and should have civil and political rights of their own.

### What still echoes today

The ideas of the age helped inspire the American and French Revolutions. In 1791, enslaved people in Haiti rose up. Their revolt forced revolutionary France to abolish slavery in 1794, and showed that the rights of man had to be claimed by those left out of them.

Human rights, free speech, and a free press are still argued in the language this century created. So is the question of who gets left out of "everyone".

## Era page: Europe and Russia in the 19th century

**Europe and Russia in the 19th century** · about 1800–1900 · Schopenhauer, John Stuart Mill, George Eliot, Dostoevsky, Tolstoy, Nietzsche

### In one line

Factories, revolutions, and new science shook the old certainties, and thinkers asked what a person should live by once faith, rank, and tradition no longer held.

### How people lived

In Britain, large numbers of people left the land for the factory towns. Before 1833, some cotton mills ran up to 71 hours a week, and children often started work at four or five. Crowded, dirty housing helped spread cholera and typhoid. Laws slowly capped working hours and banned young children from the mines.

In 1848, revolutions broke out in more than fifty countries. People demanded a voice in government, a free press, and rights for workers. Most of the uprisings were crushed, but some reforms lasted, such as the end of serfdom in Austria and Hungary.

In Russia, the tsar feared the revolutions would spread. In 1849 the young Dostoevsky was arrested for taking part in a circle that discussed banned books. He and the others were led out to be shot and pardoned only at the last moment, in a scene staged on the tsar's orders. He spent the next years in prison and exile.

### What they believed

Most Europeans were still Christian, but faith was under pressure. In 1859 Darwin published *On the Origin of Species*, which argued that living things evolve through natural selection. It clashed with the belief that each species was designed and fixed.

In 1882 Nietzsche wrote that "God is dead". He did not mean a being had died. He meant that belief in the Christian God, which had long given Europe its morals and meaning, was fading, and that people had not yet faced what that would mean.

### Who counted, and who was left out

In Russia, millions of peasants were serfs, bound to the land and to their masters. In 1861 the tsar freed more than 23 million of them. Most then had to pay for their land over 49 years.

Women had few legal rights, and no vote. Mary Ann Evans published her novels as George Eliot, in part so they would be judged on their merits and not as "women's writing".

### Ahead of their time

The law gave husbands power over their wives, and most people saw this as the natural order. In 1869, in *The Subjection of Women*, Mill called it wrong and argued for full equality between the sexes. He even compared the position of married women under the law to slavery.

Living with a man outside marriage broke the rules of Victorian society. The writer George Henry Lewes could not divorce his wife under English law, so George Eliot lived with him openly, as his wife in all but name, from 1854 until his death in 1878. It shocked many, and her own brother cut her off. She refused to hide it.

### What still echoes today

The great questions of this century were moral ones. If the old rules no longer hold, what should we live by? Dostoevsky and Tolstoy looked for an answer in faith and conscience, Mill in freedom and fairness, and Nietzsche in the courage to create new values.

We still live with their questions, in a world of fast change, fading traditions, and choices that are more and more our own.

## Era page: America in the 19th century

**America in the 19th century** · about 1830–1900 · Emerson, Margaret Fuller, Thoreau, Emily Dickinson, William James

### In one line

A young country that preached freedom while holding millions in slavery, where a small circle of New England writers told people to trust their own conscience over custom and the crowd.

### How people lived

The United States grew fast and pushed west. Under the Indian Removal Act of 1830, somewhere between 60,000 and 100,000 Native Americans were forced from their lands in the South-East. About 15,000 of them died on the way, on what became known as the Trail of Tears.

In 1860 about four million people were enslaved, nearly one American in eight. The country went to war with itself from 1861 to 1865. Slavery was abolished by the Thirteenth Amendment in December 1865.

### What they believed

Most Americans were Protestant Christians. In 1836, near Boston, Emerson and a few friends began meeting to discuss what they found lacking in the churches and colleges of their day. Critics called them "Transcendentalists", and the name stuck.

Emerson's essay *Nature*, published that same year, became their manifesto. Their message, in short: look for truth in your own experience and in nature, not only in books and pulpits.

### Who counted, and who was left out

Women could not vote, and few had a public voice.

In 1848, about 300 people met at Seneca Falls, New York, for the first women's rights convention. The call for women's right to vote was the most debated resolution. Frederick Douglass, the only African American present, spoke in favour.

### Ahead of their time

The law of the land allowed slavery and backed a war of conquest against Mexico. Thoreau argued that a person's conscience can outrank the law. In 1846 he refused to pay a tax that might help fund a war he opposed and a system that allowed slavery, and spent a night in jail. His essay on it, now known as *Civil Disobedience*, later inspired Gandhi and Martin Luther King Jr.

Women were expected to keep to the home. Margaret Fuller edited the Transcendentalists' journal, *The Dial*, and in 1845 published *Woman in the Nineteenth Century*, which urged women to seek independence through education and work. It is often called the first major feminist book in the United States.

### What still echoes today

These writers asked how a person keeps their integrity inside a society they disagree with. Thoreau's answer was quiet refusal. Fuller's was to speak up in public.

Emily Dickinson's answer was a private world of words. She wrote nearly 1,800 poems, but only ten were published in her lifetime, altered to fit the taste of the day. The rest were collected only after her death, from 1890.

Their ideas about self-reliance, nature, and conscience shaped American culture, and the question they raised is still with us: when should you follow the rules, and when should you follow your own judgement?

## Era page: Asia meets the West

**Asia meets the West** · late 19th–early 20th century · Vivekananda, Rabindranath Tagore, Okakura Kakuzō

### In one line

As Western empires and Western ideas pressed on Asia, three thinkers spoke to the West in its own language, and asked it to listen as well as teach.

### How people lived

India was ruled by Britain. Famine struck again and again. About 15 million people died in major famines between 1850 and 1899, and at least 5.6 million in the Great Famine of 1876–1878 alone.

Japan took another path. In 1853 American warships arrived and forced it to open its ports, and soon after it had to accept unequal trade treaties. In 1868 a new government took power in the emperor's name. It abolished the samurai class, built schools, factories, and an army, and borrowed Western science under the slogan "rich country, strong army".

### What they believed

India and Japan faced the same question: what to take from the West, and what to keep. Japan's new public schools taught lessons shaped by the European Enlightenment, alongside Western science and technology.

The three thinkers of this era answered by bringing Asian ideas to the West. Vivekananda taught Vedanta and yoga to Western audiences. Tagore translated his own poems into English. Okakura wrote *The Book of Tea* in English in 1906, linking the way of tea to Japanese life and art, and pushing back against Western clichés about "the East".

### Who counted, and who was left out

Under colonial rule, Indians had little say in how their own country was run. Tagore openly called for independence from Britain.

In 1913 Tagore became the first non-European to win the Nobel Prize in Literature. In 1919, after the Jallianwala Bagh massacre in Amritsar, he gave back his British knighthood.

### Ahead of their time

Many in the West saw Asian cultures as backward, and judged a nation by its strength at war. Okakura turned this around in *The Book of Tea*: the West called Japan barbarous while it practised the gentle arts of peace, and civilised only once it won a bloody war in Manchuria.

For many Indians, nationalism was the road to freedom. Tagore wanted independence too, but he was critical of nationalism and the walls it builds between countries. In his novel *Ghare Baire* (The Home and the World), he set a calm reformer against a fiery nationalist leader.

### What still echoes today

These three did not reject the West. They wrote and spoke in English and went to meet Western audiences. But each pushed back against the idea that Asia had only things to learn.

On 11 September 1893, at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago, Vivekananda greeted the audience as his sisters and brothers. About 7,000 people gave him a standing ovation.

The question these three left is still open: how do you stay rooted in your own culture while staying open to the world?

## Era page: Europe in the age of world wars

**Europe in the age of world wars** · about 1914–1945 · Simone Weil

### In one line

Thirty years of total war, mass murder, and broken faith in progress, when philosophers asked what a human life is worth and what we owe one another.

### Why only one thinker here

This era produced many of the thinkers people know best today, among them Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir, and Camus. Their works are still protected by copyright, so the app cannot yet share their words the way it shares older texts. Simone Weil stands here alone for that reason, not because she was the only voice of her time. We will add others as their works enter the public domain.

### How people lived

The First World War, from 1914 to 1918, killed somewhere between 15 and 22 million people, soldiers and civilians. France alone lost about 1.7 million, more than 4 percent of its population.

The peace did not last. The Second World War killed somewhere between 60 and 85 million people, about 3 percent of everyone alive in 1940.

### What they believed

After the slaughter of 1914–1918, the belief that the world was steadily getting better was hard to hold. People looked for meaning in politics, in faith, or in neither.

Simone Weil, born in Paris in 1909 to a secular Jewish family, tried more than one of these paths. She studied philosophy at the École Normale Supérieure, taught, and was a pacifist until the rise of the Nazis changed her mind.

### Who counted, and who was left out

Between 1941 and 1945, Nazi Germany and its allies murdered around six million Jews, about two-thirds of the Jews of Europe. Millions of others were also persecuted and killed. In 1942 Weil left France with her family for the United States, then went to London to work for the Free French.

Women in France did not gain the right to vote until 1944, and first voted in 1945. Until 1965, a married Frenchwoman needed her husband's consent to take a job.

### Ahead of their time

Few philosophers had seen factory work from the inside. In 1934, Weil took a year off teaching to work, unknown, on factory assembly lines, including at Renault, to learn what that labour does to a person. In 1936 she went to Spain to join an anarchist unit in the civil war, until an accident forced her home.

Since the French Revolution, European politics had been built on the language of rights. Weil argued that obligations come first: a right means something only when others do their duty to respect it. In 1943, as de Gaulle's Free French drafted a new declaration of the rights of man, she wrote a draft statement of human obligations instead, and set out her view in *The Need for Roots*.

### What still echoes today

Weil died in England in 1943, aged 34, of heart failure, while ill with tuberculosis and eating no more than she believed people in occupied France were given. Almost all her major works were published only after her death. Her questions about attention, the dignity of work, and the need for roots are still ours.

The questions of this era sit under much of today's thinking about human rights, about work, and about how ordinary people can be drawn into great evil.
