## Emily Dickinson

**Emily Dickinson** · Amherst, United States · 1830 – 1886 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who she was

Dickinson was born in Amherst, Massachusetts, into a prominent family. She studied at Amherst Academy for seven years and spent a short time at Mount Holyoke Female Seminary, then came back home, where she lived most of her life.

She wrote nearly 1,800 poems, but only about ten were published while she was alive. Few people around her knew she wrote at all. After her death, her younger sister Lavinia found the poems in her desk.

### What she cared about

Her poems are short, with sharp lines, dashes, and rhymes that do not quite rhyme. Her favourite subjects:

- **Nature,** watched closely, in small details.
- **Death,** a subject she came back to again and again.
- **Kindness:** a life is not wasted if it eases one person's pain.
- **Seeing differently:** what the crowd calls madness can be the deepest sense.
- **Wanting:** we understand success best when we do not have it.

### What might surprise you

The first book of her poems, in 1890, was heavily edited to fit the taste of the time. Only in 1955 did a complete edition print the poems close to how she wrote them, dashes and odd capitals included.

Early critics dismissed her as an eccentric recluse who broke the rules of grammar. At least eleven of her poems were dedicated to her sister-in-law, Susan, and some scholars read their bond as a romance.

### Where to start reading

*Poems, Series One* (1890): the first selection, short and free online. It is the edited version, so read it knowing some lines were changed.

## Epicurus

**Epicurus** · Samos, Greece · 341 – 270 BCE · Epicureanism

### Who he was

Epicurus was born on the island of Samos to Athenian parents. Around the age of thirty he began teaching in Mytilene, where his ideas caused enough trouble that he had to leave. He taught next in Lampsacus, then settled in Athens around 306 BCE.

There he bought a garden and founded his school in it. Friends, students, and women lived and studied together and shared their goods. He stayed in Athens for the rest of his life.

### What he cared about

Epicurus wanted to free people from the fears that spoil their lives. His main ideas:

- **Pleasure is the goal,** but real pleasure means no pain in the body and no trouble in the mind.
- **Some desires are natural and necessary, others are empty:** wealth and fame never satisfy.
- **Death is nothing to us:** when we are, death is not; when death is, we are not.
- **A wise, honest, and just life** is the only way to live pleasantly.
- **Friends** are the greatest help to a happy life.

### What might surprise you

He was said to have written over 300 works, and almost all are lost. What survives is three letters and some short sayings, plus pieces of his great work *On Nature*, found on burnt scrolls in Herculaneum, the town buried by Vesuvius.

He died in great pain from a blocked urinary tract. Yet his last letter to a friend calls it a happy day: against his suffering, he wrote, he set the joy of remembering their conversations.

### Where to start reading

The *Letter to Menoeceus*: a few pages that hold his whole view of a good life. R. D. Hicks's 1910 translation is free online.

## Plato

**Plato** · Athens, Greece · about 428 – 347 BCE · Classical Greek Philosophy

### Who he was

Plato was born into an old, influential Athenian family. As a young man he hoped for a life in politics. But in 404 BCE, after Athens lost its long war with Sparta, a brutal regime known as the Thirty Tyrants took power, and two of his own relatives were among them. He refused to join them.

Five years later, the restored democracy put his teacher Socrates on trial and sentenced him to death. Plato turned to philosophy instead. Around 383 BCE he founded the Academy in Athens, where the young Aristotle came to study. He died in Athens around 347 BCE.

### What he cared about

Plato wrote dialogues, not treatises, and Socrates is the main speaker in almost all of them. Their big questions:

- **The examined life:** a life without questioning is not worth living.
- **Knowing what you don't know** is where wisdom begins.
- **Justice in the soul:** a good person is at peace with himself, with reason in charge.
- **The Forms:** behind the changing world we see stand perfect, unchanging ideas.
- **Love** as the longing for what we lack, and in the end for the good itself.

### What might surprise you

Plato never speaks in his own voice. He lets Socrates and others argue, so readers still debate which views were truly his. He even had Socrates warn that writing weakens memory, in a book that has lasted 2,400 years.

He also tried to put philosophy into politics. Three times he travelled to Syracuse, in Sicily, hoping to guide its rulers. The first tyrant turned against him, and the second never became the philosopher king Plato hoped for. A scroll from Herculaneum, read in 2024, even suggests he was once sold into slavery on the island of Aegina.

### Where to start reading

The *Apology*: Socrates' speech at his trial. Short, clear, and moving. Benjamin Jowett's 1871 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Boethius

**Boethius** · Rome, Italy · about 480 – 524 CE · Stoicism

### Who he was

Boethius was born in Rome to a noble family, a few years after the last Western Roman emperor was forced from his throne. Italy was now ruled by Theodoric, king of the Ostrogoths. Boethius rose fast: senator by 25, consul by 33, and later one of the king's closest advisers.

He also set out to translate the works of Plato and Aristotle into Latin, so that Western readers could study them. In 523 he publicly defended a fellow senator accused of conspiracy and fell from power himself. He was imprisoned in Pavia on a charge of treason and executed in 524.

### What he cared about

In prison, he wrote *The Consolation of Philosophy*. In it, Philosophy appears to him as a woman and questions him about his despair. Her lessons:

- **Fortune is a wheel:** it lifts you up and drops you down, and you cannot stop it.
- **Happiness is within,** not in wealth, office, or fame.
- **True friends** are the most precious of riches.
- **Nature needs little,** and very little of that.
- **Nothing is wretched but thinking makes it so.**

### What might surprise you

Boethius was one of the leading Christian scholars of his century, yet in the *Consolation* it is Philosophy, not religion, who comes to comfort him. That is one reason the app reads him with the Stoics.

It became one of the most copied books of the Middle Ages. King Alfred translated it into Old English, and Chaucer and Queen Elizabeth I translated it too. He is honoured today as a martyr and saint in Pavia, though some historians doubt that he died for his faith.

### Where to start reading

*The Consolation of Philosophy*, Book II, where Philosophy explains Fortune. H. R. James's 1897 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## La Rochefoucauld

**François de La Rochefoucauld** · Paris, France · 1613 – 1680 · Humanists and Essayists

### Who he was

La Rochefoucauld was born in Paris into one of the greatest noble families of France. He was married at fourteen and commanding a regiment at sixteen. As a young courtier he plotted against the powerful Cardinal Richelieu, which earned him eight days in the Bastille and spells of exile from court.

In the 1640s and 1650s he joined the Fronde, a rebellion of nobles against the crown, and was badly wounded in the fighting. After that he withdrew from politics, wrote his memoirs, and became a regular of the Paris literary salons.

### What he cared about

In 1665 he published, anonymously, his *Maxims*: in the end 504 short sayings, most just two or three lines. They look hard at why people really act:

- **Self-love:** much of what we call virtue is self-interest in disguise.
- **Disguise:** we hide from others for so long that in the end we hide from ourselves.
- **Love and friendship** are rarely as pure as we claim.
- **Hypocrisy:** even false virtue is a tribute, since hypocrisy is the homage vice pays to virtue.

### What might surprise you

Many readers find the *Maxims* cynical, even bitter. Yet they grew out of a social game. In the salon of the Marquise de Sablé, guests polished short sayings together, and La Rochefoucauld kept revising his for the rest of his life.

He also tried to escape his own reputation: when a version of his memoirs appeared without his consent, he denied it was genuine, but few people believed him.

### Where to start reading

The *Maxims*: open them anywhere and read three or four at a time. The 1871 translation by J. W. Willis Bund and J. Hain Friswell is free on Project Gutenberg.

## David Hume

**David Hume** · Edinburgh, Scotland · 1711 – 1776 · Enlightenment Ethics

### Who he was

Hume was born in Edinburgh. In his twenties he wrote *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40), an attempt at a science of human nature. It sold so badly that he later said it "fell dead-born from the press". He bounced back, by his own account thanks to a cheerful temper.

His reputation for atheism twice cost him a university chair. He made his name instead as an essayist and historian: his six-volume *History of England* was a bestseller. He worked as a librarian in Edinburgh, then at the British embassy in Paris, and died in Edinburgh in 1776.

### What he cared about

Hume wanted to base philosophy on experience, not on ideas we are supposed to be born with. His key points:

- **Reason serves the passions:** our feelings set our goals; reason only finds the way.
- **Habit guides us:** we expect the future to be like the past, though we cannot prove it.
- **Belief should match the evidence,** which made him doubt reports of miracles.
- **Beauty is in the mind** of the one who looks.
- **Stay human:** be a philosopher, but do not forget to live.

### What might surprise you

Hume was famous for his good nature and was a close friend of Adam Smith. Yet his kindness to Rousseau, whom he brought to England, ended in a public quarrel, and Hume felt he had to publish his side of the story.

There is also a darker side. In a footnote to his essay "Of National Characters" (1753), he wrote racist claims about non-white peoples. Scholars who admire his work call that note shocking.

### Where to start reading

*An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*: one of the two late works he judged his greatest achievements. Free online.

## Arthur Schopenhauer

**Arthur Schopenhauer** · Danzig, Poland · 1788 – 1860 · Existential Thought

### Who he was

Schopenhauer was born in Danzig, today's Gdańsk, into a wealthy merchant family, and his inheritance earned him more each year than a professor's salary. He gave up a merchant apprenticeship to study, and published his main work, *The World as Will and Representation*, in 1818.

He tried to teach in Berlin, and scheduled his lectures at the same hour as those of Hegel, the most famous philosopher of the day, whom he called a charlatan. From 1833 he lived alone in Frankfurt, with a series of pet poodles, and recognition came late: in his final years, admirers bought his portrait and visited the places he had lived.

### What he cared about

Schopenhauer is known as the philosopher of pessimism. Behind everything, he saw a blind, restless will that keeps us wanting. His ideas:

- **Life swings between pain and boredom;** happiness is mostly the absence of both.
- **What you are inside** matters more for happiness than what you have or what others think of you.
- **Riches and fame are like sea water:** the more you drink, the thirstier you get.
- **Compassion** is the root of morality: we feel another's suffering as our own.
- **Think for yourself:** reading is thinking with someone else's head.

### What might surprise you

He was one of the first Western philosophers to take Indian thought seriously. He called the Upanishads the solace of his life, and named one of his poodles Atman, the Sanskrit word for the self.

He also held openly hostile views of women. His 1851 essay "On Women" mocked what he saw as a foolish reverence for women, and it still feeds sexist views today. He later admitted, after meeting the sculptor Elisabet Ney, that he had not yet said his last word on the subject.

### Where to start reading

*The Wisdom of Life* and *Counsels and Maxims*: short, practical essays on happiness, far easier than his main work. T. Bailey Saunders's translations are free on Project Gutenberg.

## Epictetus

**Epictetus** · Hierapolis, Turkey · about 50 – 135 CE · Stoicism

### Who he was

Epictetus was born a slave in Hierapolis, in today's Turkey. He grew up in Rome as the slave of Epaphroditus, a wealthy secretary to the emperor Nero. At some point he became lame: one ancient writer claims his master broke his leg, another that he was disabled from childhood.

He studied Stoic philosophy with Musonius Rufus and, once freed, began to teach. When the emperor Domitian banished philosophers from Rome, Epictetus moved to Nicopolis, in north-west Greece, and opened a school there. He lived simply, with few possessions, until his death.

### What he cared about

For Epictetus, philosophy is a way of life, not just a theory. His teaching rests on one idea:

- **Some things are in our power, others are not.** Our judgments, choices, and desires are ours; our body, reputation, and fortune are not.
- **Freedom** is living as we choose, inside what is truly ours.
- **Every event has two handles:** take hold of it by the one you can carry.
- **Responsibility:** we answer for our own actions, through daily self-discipline.

### What might surprise you

Epictetus wrote nothing himself. Everything we have comes from his student Arrian, who took down his lessons: the *Discourses* and a short handbook, the *Enchiridion*.

The former slave went on to shape an emperor: Marcus Aurelius was among the many readers he influenced. In old age, the man who lived alone took in a friend's child who would otherwise have been left to die, and raised him.

### Where to start reading

The *Enchiridion*: a short handbook, the essence of his teaching in a few pages. George Long's 1877 translation is free on Project Gutenberg.

## Rumi

**Rumi** · Wakhsh, Tajikistan · 1207 – 1273 · Persian and Sufi Poetry

### Who he was

Jalal al-Din Rumi was born to Persian parents in Wakhsh, in today's Tajikistan. While he was still a child, the advancing Mongols drove his family west, through Iran, Baghdad, and Damascus. At nineteen he settled with them in Konya, in today's Turkey, a land that had once been Roman, which is where his name, "the Roman", comes from.

Like his father, he became a respected Islamic scholar and teacher. Then, in 1244, he met a wandering dervish, Shams of Tabriz, and his life changed. The two became inseparable. When Shams mysteriously disappeared, Rumi's grief poured out as poetry, and he kept writing until his death in Konya in 1273.

### What he cared about

- **Love** as the force that moves everything, above all the longing of the soul for God.
- **Separation and longing:** the Masnavi opens with a reed flute, his favourite instrument, singing of separation.
- **Stories that teach:** his long poem, the *Masnavi*, is full of fables and everyday scenes.
- **Music and movement** as ways to reach the divine.

### What might surprise you

Rumi is often called the world's most popular poet, and in recent years he has been one of the best-selling poets in the United States. But many popular English versions are free interpretations, not close translations of the Persian.

The famous whirling dervishes grew out of his teaching: after his death, his son and followers founded the Mevlevi order. When Rumi died, Christians and Jews joined the Muslims of Konya in the crowd that mourned him.

### Where to start reading

The *Masnavi*, Book I, which opens with the song of the reed. E. H. Whinfield's 1898 abridged translation is free online.

## Aristotle

**Aristotle** · Stagira, Greece · 384 – 322 BCE · Classical Greek Philosophy

### Who he was

Aristotle was born in Stagira, in northern Greece. His father was physician to the king of Macedon, which may explain his lifelong interest in biology and medicine. Orphaned young, he was raised by a guardian, and at about eighteen he joined Plato's Academy in Athens, where he stayed for nearly twenty years.

After Plato died, he spent years studying plants and sea creatures on the island of Lesbos. In 343 BCE Philip II of Macedon asked him to tutor his son, the future Alexander the Great. Back in Athens, Aristotle founded his own school, the Lyceum, with a large library.

### What he cared about

Aristotle wrote on almost everything, from logic and physics to poetry and politics. On how to live, his main ideas:

- **Flourishing** (*eudaimonia*) is the goal of life: a whole life lived well.
- **We become what we do:** just by doing just acts, brave by doing brave ones.
- **Friendship** at its best is between good people who love each other for who they are.
- **Humans are social by nature;** we flourish only in a community with laws.
- **Good laws** are reason free from passion.

### What might surprise you

Only about a third of what he wrote has survived, and none of it was meant for publication. The polished dialogues he wrote for readers are lost; what we read are works he never prepared for the public.

For centuries he was simply "the First Teacher" to Muslim scholars and "the Philosopher" to Christian ones like Thomas Aquinas. Yet he also argued, in his *Politics*, that some people are slaves by nature, a view later used to defend slavery.

### Where to start reading

The *Nicomachean Ethics*, Books I and II: what a good life is, and how good character is built. Free online in older translations.
